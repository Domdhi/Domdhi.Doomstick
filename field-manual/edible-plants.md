# Edible Wild Plants — Continental United States

**Source:** USDA Agricultural Research Service and USDA Forest Service publications. US Government works, public domain per 17 U.S.C. § 105.

---

## CRITICAL DISCLAIMER

**Positive identification is a matter of life and death.** Some of the most toxic plants in North America closely resemble edible species. This document provides identification guidance based on USDA publications, but a text description is not a substitute for:

1. Hands-on training with a qualified botanist or experienced forager
2. Cross-referencing multiple identification features (never rely on one characteristic alone)
3. Consulting multiple field guides specific to your geographic region
4. Local knowledge of what species are actually present in your area

**When in doubt, do not eat.** The hunger you experience in a survival situation will not kill you as quickly as plant poisoning. The Universal Edibility Test (Chapter 1) provides a systematic approach for unknown plants, but even it does not catch all toxins.

---

## Chapter 1: The Universal Edibility Test

The Universal Edibility Test is a 9-step systematic procedure derived from US Army survival training (FM 21-76) for testing an unknown plant for edibility when positive identification is not possible. The test takes approximately 24 hours to complete for one plant part.

**Critical rules before beginning:**
- Test only one plant part at a time (root, stem, leaf, flower, seed separately)
- Test only one plant at a time
- Eat only small quantities of other known safe foods during the test period
- Drink only clean water during the test period

---

### Step 1: Separate the Plant Parts

Divide the plant into its components:
- Roots and root-like structures (tubers, bulbs, rhizomes)
- Bark, stems, and stalks
- Leaves
- Flowers
- Seeds and grains
- Fruit

Test each part separately. A plant may have edible leaves but toxic roots, or edible fruit but toxic seeds. Do not assume that edibility of one part implies edibility of another.

---

### Step 2: Smell Test

Crush or bruise a small portion of the plant part being tested.

**Odors that are immediate warning signs — do not proceed:**
- Bitter almond smell: Indicates hydrogen cyanide (cyanogenic glycosides). Found in: bitter almonds, apricot pits, elderberry leaves, many Prunus species (cherry, plum) leaves and bark.
- Strong, acrid, or irritating smell: Many irritant plants produce harsh odors.
- Soapy smell: Some toxic plants (saponins).
- Petroleum-like smell: Chemical contamination.

**A neutral or pleasant smell does not guarantee safety.** Deadly nightshade berries smell pleasant. Death camas has a faintly sweet smell. The smell test eliminates some plants; passing it does not confirm safety.

---

### Step 3: Contact Test — Skin

With the crushed plant material still on your fingers, rub the material against the inside of your wrist or the crook of your elbow. These areas have thin, sensitive skin.

Wait 15 minutes. If any of the following occur, discard the plant:
- Redness (erythema)
- Burning or stinging sensation
- Hives or raised bumps (urticarial response)
- Blistering (rare but a hard stop)
- Itching that does not resolve

**Note:** This test identifies contact irritants but not all systemic toxins. Passing this test does not confirm internal safety.

---

### Step 4: Prepare the Plant

Prepare the plant part as you would for eating: if it is something that would normally be cooked, cook a small portion. Cooking deactivates some toxins (notably protease inhibitors in beans, some glycoalkaloids in potatoes, and reduces oxalic acid). However, cooking does NOT neutralize: alkaloids (like those in hemlock or belladonna), cardiac glycosides (foxglove), cyanogenic glycosides (elderberry raw seeds/bark), or most heavy metal contamination.

---

### Step 5: Lip Test

Place a small piece of the prepared plant on your lips. Do not put it inside your mouth. Wait 3 minutes.

Discard if: burning, stinging, numbness, or tingling occurs.

**Numbness or tingling is a particularly important warning:** Many toxic plants produce these sensations (water hemlock, aconite/monkshood, arum relatives like jack-in-the-pulpit before cooking).

---

### Step 6: Tongue Test

Place a small piece on the tip of your tongue. Do not chew. Do not swallow. Wait 15 minutes.

Discard if: any burning, stinging, numbness, tingling, excessive salivation, or strong bitterness.

---

### Step 7: Chew and Hold

Chew the small piece thoroughly. Hold it in your mouth for 15 minutes without swallowing.

Discard if: any burning, stinging, or other reaction.

**Important:** Some reactions take time to develop. The full 15 minutes are required.

---

### Step 8: Swallow and Wait

Swallow the chewed material. Wait 8 hours. During this time, drink water only. Do not eat other foods (this can mask or confuse reaction symptoms). Resume normal activity.

Discard if: within those 8 hours you experience:
- Nausea
- Vomiting
- Diarrhea
- Abdominal cramping
- Dizziness
- Weakness
- Rash or skin reaction
- Difficulty breathing
- Altered consciousness

**If you experience vomiting or severe diarrhea:** Induce additional vomiting only if the plant material was consumed recently and you cannot get medical care. Activated charcoal (if available) can bind many plant toxins. Get to medical care as rapidly as possible.

---

### Step 9: Larger Portion Test

If you have had no reaction after 8 hours, eat approximately a quarter cup of the plant (prepared the same way). Wait another 8 hours. If no reaction, the plant part tested is likely safe for you to eat.

**Caveats:**
- "Likely safe" is not "definitely safe." Individual sensitivities vary. Some toxins accumulate over multiple exposures.
- This test result applies to that plant part, from that plant. Do not generalize to other plant parts or to similar-looking plants.
- The test does not assess long-term toxicity. Some plant toxins are harmful with repeated consumption but not in a single test quantity.

## Chapter 2: Safe Wild Plants

The following plants are among the most widely distributed, easily identified edible wild plants in the continental United States. Each entry includes identification features, edible parts, preparation, and toxic lookalikes.

---

### 2.1 Dandelion (Taraxacum officinale)

**Family:** Asteraceae
**Distribution:** Throughout the continental US, all seasons (best in spring and fall)

**Identification:**
- Leaves arise from the base in a rosette pattern; leaves are toothed (the teeth point backward toward the base of the leaf — hence "lion's tooth," the French origin of the name)
- Hollow stem, single yellow composite flower per stem
- White, fluffy seed head (dandelion "clock") when mature
- When stem or leaf is broken, milky white sap appears
- All parts are edible; roots are large and fleshy (like a thick pencil at the base)

**Edible parts and preparation:**
- **Leaves (best in spring before flowering):** Eat raw in salads or cook like spinach. Young spring leaves are least bitter; mature or summer leaves become very bitter. Blanching in boiling water for 30 seconds, then discarding the water, reduces bitterness significantly.
- **Flowers:** Eat raw, use in fritters (dip in batter and fry), or make into tea. A good source of vitamins A, B, C.
- **Root:** Eat raw (chew and spit out the stringy core) or roast to use as a coffee substitute. Roasted dandelion root is a traditional herbal beverage.
- **Nutritional value:** Rich in vitamins A, C, K; calcium; iron; potassium. One of the most nutritionally dense wild plants.

**Toxic lookalikes:** None. Dandelion is essentially impossible to confuse with anything toxic if you check: single stem per flower, hollow stem, milky sap, rosette leaves. Cat's ear (Hypochaeris) looks similar but has branched stems and is also edible.

---

### 2.2 Cattail (Typha latifolia, T. angustifolia, T. domingensis)

**Family:** Typhaceae
**Distribution:** Wetlands, marshes, pond edges, ditches throughout the US; multiple seasons

**Identification:**
- Tall (1-3 meters), grass-like leaves, flat and strap-like, rising from the water or wet soil
- Distinctive brown, sausage-shaped seedhead (the "cat tail") atop a stiff stalk
- Young shoots emerging from rhizomes in spring are white at the base
- Plants form dense colonies in shallow water

**Edible parts and preparation:**
- **Shoots/Corms (spring):** Pull the young shoot up and peel back the outer leaves. The tender white inner shoot (lower 30-40 cm) has a mild flavor similar to cucumber. Eat raw or lightly cooked.
- **Pollen (late spring/early summer):** The immature male flower spike (above the brown female spike) produces large quantities of bright yellow pollen. Shake or strip into a bag. Use as a high-protein flour supplement (mix with wheat flour for bread or pancakes). Harvest before the pollen disperses (while the male spike is still tightly covered by a leafy sheath).
- **Immature green female spike (late spring):** Before the brown color develops, the female spike can be stripped and eaten like corn on the cob, boiled or raw.
- **Rhizomes (fall and winter):** The starchy underground rhizome can be processed for starch. Dig, wash, peel, crush in water, allow the starch to settle, pour off the water, and use the starch like flour. Labor-intensive but calorie-dense.

**Toxic lookalikes:** None in the US. Young cattail shoots could theoretically be confused with iris shoots. Iris leaves are narrower with a distinctive midrib (cattail leaves are flat/solid without a midrib). Iris has purple or yellow flowers, never a cattail-type seedhead. Iris is toxic. Always confirm the cattail seedhead or shoot from an established plant before harvesting shoots.

---

### 2.3 Wood Sorrel (Oxalis stricta, O. acetosella, and related species)

**Family:** Oxalidaceae
**Distribution:** Throughout the US, in woodlands, disturbed soil, gardens

**Identification:**
- Trefoil leaves — three heart-shaped leaflets attached at a center point (looks like clover but leaflets are heart-shaped, not oval)
- Leaves fold downward at night and in intense sunlight
- Yellow or white small flowers (depending on species)
- Seed pods look like tiny okra, elongated and pointed

**Edible parts and preparation:**
- **Leaves, flowers, seed pods:** All edible raw. The characteristic sour flavor comes from oxalic acid.
- Excellent for adding a lemony flavor to salads, soups, or as a trail snack.
- Nutritionally: high in vitamin C.

**Important limitation:** Oxalic acid binds calcium and in very large quantities can cause kidney problems. Eating large amounts daily is not advisable. In a survival situation, normal quantities are safe.

**Not to be confused with:** Clover (Trifolium) — similar trefoil leaves but oval leaflets without the heart-shaped notch at the tip, and different flowers. Clover is also edible but lacks the sorrel's sour flavor.

---

### 2.4 Purslane (Portulaca oleracea)

**Family:** Portulacaceae
**Distribution:** Disturbed soil, gardens, fields, roadsides throughout the US; summer

**Identification:**
- Low-growing, succulent (thick, fleshy), sprawling plant
- Reddish, smooth stems
- Small, spatula-shaped (paddle-shaped), thick, fleshy leaves
- Tiny yellow flowers (5 petals) that open only in bright sun
- Grows in disturbed, often dry soil; common garden weed

**Edible parts and preparation:**
- **Leaves, stems, and flowers:** All edible raw or cooked. The mucilaginous texture makes it useful as a soup thickener.
- Raw: mild flavor, slightly tangy, used in salads.
- Cooked: wilts quickly; use like spinach, added to stews or stir-fried.
- **Nutritional value:** One of the richest plant sources of omega-3 fatty acids. Also high in vitamins A, C, and magnesium.

**Toxic lookalikes:** Hairy-stemmed spurge (Euphorbia species) — grows in similar habitats but has hairy stems (purslane is smooth) and milky white sap when broken (purslane has clear or no sap). Spurge is toxic.

---

### 2.5 Lamb's Quarters (Chenopodium album)

**Family:** Amaranthaceae (formerly Chenopodiaceae)
**Distribution:** Throughout the US, in gardens, disturbed soil, fields; spring through fall

**Identification:**
- Upright plant, typically 30 cm to 1.5 meters tall
- Leaves are triangular to diamond-shaped with irregular, toothed margins
- Young leaves and stem tips have a distinctive powdery, mealy white coating (like flour) — this is the key identifying feature
- Older leaves are dark green on top, lighter below
- In late summer: dense clusters of tiny green seed heads along the stem

**Edible parts and preparation:**
- **Young leaves and shoot tips (best in spring):** Eat raw in salads or cook like spinach. The mealy coating disappears when cooked.
- **Seeds (late summer/fall):** Related to quinoa; seeds are nutritious and can be used as a grain. Rinse thoroughly before cooking to remove saponins.
- **Nutritional value:** High in vitamins A and C, calcium, iron, and protein.

**Toxic lookalikes:** None truly toxic, but be careful to distinguish from:
- Nightshade species (Solanum) — lack the mealy coating; have purple or white star-shaped flowers
- Always confirm the white mealy coating on young leaves; this feature is unique to Chenopodium

---

### 2.6 Violet (Viola species)

**Family:** Violaceae
**Distribution:** Throughout the US in a variety of habitats; spring

**Identification:**
- Heart-shaped leaves with finely toothed margins
- Distinctive flowers: 5 petals, usually violet-purple or white, bilaterally symmetrical; the two upper petals differ from the three lower petals
- Low-growing; flowers and leaves arise directly from the ground or on separate short stems from a central crown
- Common in moist, shaded woodland areas

**Edible parts and preparation:**
- **Leaves:** Eat raw in salads or cooked. Young spring leaves are best — tender and mild.
- **Flowers:** Eat raw; high in vitamin C. Use in salads, as a garnish, or to make violet jelly or vinegar.
- **Leaves as a thickener:** Violet leaves contain mucilage and can be added to soups to thicken them (similar to okra).

**Toxic lookalikes:** None among common species that closely resemble violet. Arum lily (Arisaema species) and trillium grow in similar habitats but have dramatically different leaf shapes. Wild ginger (Asarum) has heart-shaped leaves but grows along the ground with a distinctive ginger odor and different flowers.

---

### 2.7 Blackberries and Raspberries (Rubus species)

**Family:** Rosaceae
**Distribution:** Throughout the US in thickets, edges of forests, roadsides; fruit in summer

**Identification:**
- Thorny (prickly) canes, often arching
- Compound leaves with 3-7 leaflets, finely toothed
- White 5-petaled flowers (spring)
- Fruit: aggregate drupelets — multiple small juicy segments fused together, red (raspberry) or black (blackberry) when ripe
- Blackberry cores remain firm; raspberry cores hollow out (the berry slides off the core).

**Edible parts and preparation:**
- **Fruit (ripe):** Eat raw. Rich in vitamins C and K, manganese, and antioxidants.
- **Leaves:** Dried leaves make tea; tea has a pleasant mild flavor and contains tannins. Young leaves can be eaten raw in small quantities.
- **Young shoots (spring):** The white, tender, inner part of young canes (before they stiffen) can be peeled and eaten raw like a vegetable.

**Toxic lookalikes:** No truly toxic plants closely resemble Rubus with ripe fruit. Poke (Phytolacca americana) has dark berries but grows as a large plant with thick reddish stems and no thorns, and the berries are arranged differently (in a raceme). Elderberry (Sambucus canadensis) has compound leaves and small dark berries in flat-topped clusters — the raw berries can cause nausea, but they also lack thorns.

**Note on unripe fruit:** Unripe blackberries and raspberries contain higher levels of tannins and can cause stomach upset. Eat only fully ripe, black/dark red fruit.

---

### 2.8 Wild Onion (Allium canadense, A. cernuum, and related species)

**Family:** Amaryllidaceae
**Distribution:** Throughout the US in grasslands, open woodlands, disturbed areas

**Identification:**
- **The most critical identification feature: onion smell.** Any plant in the Allium genus will smell distinctly of onion or garlic when a leaf or bulb is crushed. This smell is caused by sulfur compounds unique to Allium. If it does not smell like onion, it is NOT a wild onion.
- Narrow, grass-like leaves (hollow in wild onion, round in cross-section)
- A true bulb at the base (smaller than cultivated onion but the same structure)
- Flowers: small, white or pink, borne in an umbel (umbrella-like cluster) at the top of the stem
- Some species produce small bulblets (tiny secondary bulbs) in the flower cluster

**Edible parts and preparation:**
- All parts (bulb, leaves, flowers) are edible and used like cultivated onion.
- Use as flavoring in soups, stews, or eat raw.
- **High in vitamins A and C.**

**CRITICAL toxic lookalike: Death Camas (Anticlea elegans, formerly Zigadenus species)**

Death camas is the most dangerous lookalike for wild onion. It causes cardiac failure and death. The key distinction:

| Feature | Wild Onion | Death Camas |
|---------|-----------|-------------|
| **Smell** | **Strong onion smell** | **No onion smell** |
| Leaves | Hollow or flat; onion smell | Solid, grass-like; no smell |
| Bulb | Onion-like structure | Bulb with papery tunic |
| Flowers | White or pink, 6 petals | Creamy white, 6 petals |
| Habitat | Similar habitats | Similar habitats |

**The smell test is absolute. If it does not smell like onion, do not eat it.** Death camas has no onion odor whatsoever. If you are in doubt, do not eat it.

---

### 2.9 Pine Needles (Pinus species) — Tea Only

**Family:** Pinaceae
**Distribution:** Throughout the US in various species

**Identification:**
- Evergreen trees with needles in bundles (fascicles): 2, 3, or 5 needles per bundle depending on species
- Distinctive pine cones
- Resinous smell when needles are crushed
- Young (lighter green, softer) needles at the branch tips

**Use:**
- **Pine needle tea:** Rich in vitamin C. Steep a small handful of young green needles in hot water for 10-15 minutes. Do not boil — boiling drives off vitamin C. Drink the tea and discard the needles.
- Inner bark (cambium, the thin moist layer between the bark and the wood) can be eaten in spring when the sap is rising. Strip with a knife and eat raw or dry and grind into flour. It is starchy and somewhat sweet.
- **Seeds (pine nuts):** Large-seeded pines (Pinus edulis — piñon, Pinus monophylla, Pinus lambertiana — sugar pine) produce edible seeds. Crack the scales of mature cones to extract.

**IMPORTANT — species to avoid:**
- **Yew (Taxus species):** Not a pine. Has flat, dark green needles arranged in two rows (not in bundles), red berry-like fruit (arils), and is highly toxic. All parts except the red aril flesh are toxic. Do not confuse with pine.
- **Norfolk Island pine (Araucaria heterophylla):** An ornamental. Not edible.
- Avoid eastern red cedar / juniper needles (Juniperus species) in large quantities — they are not toxic in small doses but contain compounds that are irritating to the kidneys in quantity.

---

### 2.10 Acorns (Quercus species) — Requires Processing

**Family:** Fagaceae
**Distribution:** Throughout the US; oaks grow in virtually every habitat

**Identification:**
- Oak trees are highly variable but all produce acorns: small nuts in a distinctive cap (cupule)
- Leaves are generally lobed (though some oaks have nearly unlobed leaves)
- Two major groups:
  - **Red/Black oaks (pointed leaf lobes):** Acorns require two seasons to mature; more bitter (higher tannins)
  - **White oaks (rounded leaf lobes):** Acorns mature in one season; less bitter

**Nutritional value:** Acorns are one of the highest-calorie wild plant foods available. High in fats, moderate protein, complex carbohydrates. Native American cultures throughout the US used acorn meal as a dietary staple.

**CRITICAL: Acorns are NOT edible raw due to tannins.** All acorns contain bitter tannins that cause intestinal distress and inhibit nutrient absorption. White oak acorns have less; red/black oak acorns have more. All require processing to be safely eaten.

**Tannin leaching — water method:**
1. Shell the acorns and chop or grind the nut meat coarsely.
2. Cover with cold water in a container.
3. Change the water every several hours (or as soon as it turns brown).
4. Continue until the water no longer turns brown and the taste is no longer bitter (taste test a small piece after each change).
5. This may take 24-48 hours with cold water changes, or can be accelerated by using hot (not boiling) water and changing more frequently (4-6 hours).
6. Dry the leached acorn meal and use like flour.

**Running water method:** Place a mesh bag of crushed acorns in a running stream for 24-48 hours. The running water continuously carries away tannins. Traditional Native American method.

**Uses:** Acorn flour for flat bread, porridge, thickening soups. Acorn porridge (mush) made by boiling the leached meal in water is the simplest preparation.

**Toxic lookalikes:** None. Acorns (with their distinctive cap) are unmistakable.

## Chapter 3: Toxic Plants to Avoid

These plants are responsible for the majority of serious wild plant poisonings in the United States. Know them well.

---

### 3.1 Water Hemlock (Cicuta maculata and related species)

**The most violently toxic plant in North America.** Even a small amount of the root can kill an adult. Called the "cowbane" for good reason — it kills livestock.

**Appearance:**
- Tall plant (1-2.5 meters) in wet areas (stream banks, marshes, ditches)
- Hollow stem with purple streaking
- Compound leaves with many lance-shaped leaflets; leaflets have distinctive "V" pattern of veins going to the leaf margin notches (not to the tips)
- White flowers in umbels (umbrella-like flat-topped clusters)
- Roots: chambered (air pockets visible when cut) with a characteristic carrot-like smell

**Toxin:** Cicutoxin. Causes violent convulsions within minutes to an hour.

**Why it's dangerous:** Water hemlock closely resembles edible plants: wild parsnip, wild parsley, water parsley, elders (Sambucus) when young, and various other Apiaceae (carrot family) relatives. If you cannot positively identify an Apiaceae member, do not eat it.

**Key ID features:**
- Hollow, chambered root (not solid)
- Purple or red streaking on the stem
- Wet habitat
- Carrot-like or sweet smell from the root (the smell can be appealing — this is a trap)

---

### 3.2 Poison Hemlock (Conium maculatum)

The plant that killed Socrates. Not as immediately toxic as water hemlock but still fatally poisonous.

**Appearance:**
- Tall plant (1-3 meters) with smooth, hollow stems marked with purple-red blotches or spots
- Fine, fern-like, fernlike compound leaves
- Small white flowers in umbels
- Musty, unpleasant "mousy" odor when leaves or stem are crushed — this is the most useful distinguishing feature

**Toxin:** Coniine. Causes ascending paralysis — starts in the legs and moves upward to the respiratory muscles, causing death by asphyxiation.

**Why it's dangerous:** Resembles edible relatives including wild carrot (Daucus carota), parsley, and other Apiaceae species. Young poison hemlock leaves can resemble flat-leaf parsley.

**Key ID features:**
- Smooth, hollow stem with purple/red blotches (not hairy like many edible Apiaceae)
- Musty, unpleasant smell (vs. pleasant carrot/parsley smell of edible relatives)
- Purple-blotched stem (vs. solid green of most edible relatives)

**Rule:** Do not eat any wild plant in the carrot family (Apiaceae — umbrella-shaped white flower clusters, compound leaves) unless you can positively identify it.

---

### 3.3 Death Camas (Anticlea elegans, Zigadenus venenosus, and related species)

(See also Section 2.8 — Wild Onion lookalike)

**One of the leading causes of fatal plant poisoning** when confused with wild onion or wild garlic.

**Appearance:**
- Grass-like leaves emerging from an onion-like bulb
- Creamy white to yellowish flowers in a raceme or panicle
- Grows in similar habitat to wild onion

**Toxin:** Zygacine and related steroidal alkaloids. Cause dizziness, salivation, weakness, respiratory depression, cardiac failure.

**The absolute distinguishing feature:** No onion smell. Wild onion smells like onion. Death camas does not. This single test separates them.

---

### 3.4 False Morel vs. True Morel

**True morel (Morchella species):** Edible and highly prized when cooked. Cap is a sponge-like, pitted, honeycombed surface. Cap is attached to the stem all the way around the base (no space between cap and stem). Hollow inside (cut in half lengthwise to verify).

**False morel / Brain mushroom (Gyromitra species):** Toxic raw; some species toxic even cooked. Cap is wrinkled, lobed, and brain-like (saddle-shaped or irregular), NOT pitted. Cap may be attached to the stem on one side but has a gap. Contains gyromitrin, which metabolizes to monomethylhydrazine (a rocket fuel component) — causes liver and kidney failure.

**Key distinction:**
- True morel: pitted/honeycombed cap, fully attached, hollow
- False morel: wrinkled/brain-like cap, may be partially attached, may have cottony interior

**Mushroom general note:** This document does not provide comprehensive mushroom identification guidance. Wild mushroom identification requires hands-on training and multiple field guides. Many deadly mushrooms (Amanita species — destroying angel, death cap) closely resemble edible species. **Do not eat wild mushrooms without expert instruction.**

---

### 3.5 Destroying Angel and Death Cap (Amanita bisporigera, A. phalloides)

**Responsible for the majority of fatal mushroom poisonings worldwide.** The Amanita genus contains both deadly and harmless species.

**Destroying angel (Amanita bisporigera) appearance:**
- Entirely white: white cap, white gills, white stem, white ring (skirt), white cup at base
- The cup (volva) at the base of the stem is buried in the soil — dig carefully to see it
- Smooth cap, 5-15 cm diameter
- Grows in forests, often near oaks

**Toxin:** Amatoxins (amatoxins and phallotoxins). Cause delayed-onset liver and kidney failure. Symptoms begin 6-24 hours after eating (too late for effective emesis to help). Death occurs in 5-10 days from organ failure.

**Why it's dangerous:** Has been confused with edible puffballs (before the cap opens), young edible Amanita species (some are edible), and with button mushrooms at the "egg" stage before the cap opens.

**Rule:** Never eat a white or pale mushroom with gills without expert identification. The destroying angel is the single most important plant/fungus to recognize in North American foraging.

## Chapter 4: Plant Parts and Seasonal Availability

### Spring
- **Best season for greens:** Most leafy plants are tender, less bitter, and highest in nutrients.
- Dandelion leaves, lamb's quarters (young), violet leaves and flowers, wood sorrel
- Cattail shoots, inner stems
- Wild onion bulbs and leaves
- Blackberry/raspberry young shoots

### Summer
- Purslane (peak season — a summer weed)
- Wild berries: blackberries, raspberries, blueberries, mulberries (when ripe)
- Elderberries (blue/black species — cook before eating; red elderberries are more strongly toxic)
- Cattail pollen (early summer)
- Wild onion flowering

### Fall
- Acorns (September-November, depending on species and latitude)
- Cattail rhizomes (best after frost for starch content)
- Late berries
- Wild onion bulbs

### Winter
- Cattail rhizomes (can be harvested through ice)
- Pine inner bark (thin layer) and pine needle tea year-round
- Acorns if stored or still on the ground

## Chapter 5: Preparation Guidelines

### Reducing Bitterness in Greens

Tannins, alkaloids, and other compounds cause bitterness in many wild plants. Reduce bitterness by:
- **Parboiling:** Bring water to a boil, add greens, boil 2-3 minutes, drain and discard the water. Refill with clean water and cook until tender. Repeat once for very bitter plants.
- **Young growth:** Harvest before the plant begins to flower (leaf bitterness increases as the plant shifts energy toward reproduction).
- **Cold-water soak:** Soaking greens in cold water for several hours reduces water-soluble bitter compounds.

### Leaching Tannins from Acorns

(See Section 2.10 for detailed procedure)

### Cooking to Improve Digestibility

Many plants contain compounds that are reduced or eliminated by cooking:
- **Beans and legumes:** Raw seeds contain protease inhibitors and lectins causing severe gastrointestinal distress. Boil thoroughly (minimum 10 minutes at a rolling boil after soaking overnight).
- **Pokeweed (Phytolacca americana):** Young leaves are edible only after multiple boilings with water changes. Not recommended for beginners — the margin between edible and toxic is narrow. Not covered in the safe plants chapter intentionally.
- **Jack-in-the-pulpit (Arisaema triphyllum):** The corm contains calcium oxalate crystals that cause intense burning if eaten raw. Cooking and drying reduce the crystals. Not recommended without guidance.

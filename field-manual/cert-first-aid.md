# CERT First Aid and Emergency Response

**Source:** FEMA CERT (Community Emergency Response Team) training program. US Government work, public domain per 17 U.S.C. § 105.

---

## Introduction to CERT

The Community Emergency Response Team (CERT) program is administered by the Federal Emergency Management Agency (FEMA), a component of the US Department of Homeland Security. CERT training educates community members in basic disaster response skills: fire safety, light search and rescue, and basic disaster medical operations.

CERT volunteers are trained to assist their communities during disasters when professional first responders may be overwhelmed, delayed, or inaccessible. The skills taught in CERT training are applicable to any emergency, not only declared disasters.

This document summarizes key content from the CERT training curriculum, which is a US Government work produced by a federal agency and is in the public domain.

## Chapter 1: Scene Safety and CERT Activation

### 1.1 Personal Safety First

The first principle of CERT response is that responders who become casualties cannot help anyone. Before entering any scene:

**Stop. Think. Act. Review. (STAR)**

- **Stop:** Pause before rushing in. Take 30 seconds minimum.
- **Think:** What hazards are present or likely? What do I need to assess?
- **Act:** Take the appropriate, measured action.
- **Review:** Did your action produce the intended result? Are there new hazards?

**Scene safety assessment checklist:**
- Structural integrity: Is the building stable? Are walls vertical? Are there obvious cracks, shifts, or collapses?
- Fire/smoke: Is there active fire? Smoke? Smell of gas?
- Electrical hazards: Are there downed power lines? Sparking equipment?
- Hazardous materials (HazMat): Are there chemical odors, discolored vegetation, unusual fumes? Look for placards on containers or vehicles.
- Secondary hazards: Are there unstable debris piles, broken glass, sharp objects, compromised utilities?
- Traffic and bystanders: Is the scene controlled?

**When NOT to enter:**
- Active fire that has not been fully suppressed
- Confirmed or suspected gas leaks (HazMat protocols apply)
- Structurally unsound buildings until stabilized
- Any scene where your safety cannot be reasonably assured

**If you cannot safely enter:** Establish a perimeter. Mark the area. Direct people away. Wait for properly equipped professional responders. Your job is to support, not to become a casualty.

---

### 1.2 CERT Team Activation

CERT teams activate through an established chain of command:

1. **Team Leader** assesses the overall situation and assigns functions.
2. **Search and Rescue Team** locates and extracts trapped victims.
3. **Medical Team** triages and treats casualties at the treatment area.
4. **Logistics** handles supplies, communications, and documentation.

**Documentation is always required.** The CERT team records:
- Who was in the building and where
- Actions taken and results
- Resource use (supplies, personnel)
- Handoff information for incoming professional responders

Without documentation, the handoff to professional responders is incomplete and risks duplicating efforts or missing victims.

## Chapter 2: Disaster Medical Operations — Overview

### 2.1 The Mass Casualty Incident (MCI) Challenge

In a Mass Casualty Incident (MCI), the number of casualties exceeds the immediate capacity of the available medical resources. Standard emergency room practice (treat the most seriously injured first) fails in an MCI because it would consume all available resources on a small number of patients while many others who could be saved receive no treatment.

MCI medicine inverts this principle: **treat those most likely to benefit, not those most severely injured.** The goal is to save the greatest number of lives, not to provide the ideal treatment to each individual.

This requires triage: rapid assessment and categorization of all casualties to determine who receives immediate treatment, who can wait, who cannot be saved, and who is already dead.

CERT members perform initial triage and basic treatment. Professional EMS and medical personnel perform definitive treatment and transport.

## Chapter 3: START Triage

### 3.1 What is START Triage?

START (Simple Triage And Rapid Treatment) is the standard initial triage system used by civilian emergency services in the United States. It was developed at Hoag Hospital in Newport Beach, California, and is now the basis for CERT triage training.

START uses three assessments to categorize each patient into one of four categories:

**R — Respirations (breathing)**
**P — Perfusion (circulation)**
**M — Mental Status (consciousness and cognitive function)**

The assessment takes 30-60 seconds per patient. CERT members do not provide treatment during triage — they categorize patients and move on. Stopping to treat during triage delays the assessment of other patients who may be dying.

---

### 3.2 The Four START Categories

| Color | Tag | Meaning | Priority |
|-------|-----|---------|----------|
| **RED** | Immediate | Life-threatening but potentially survivable with immediate intervention | Highest |
| **YELLOW** | Delayed | Serious injury, stable, can wait 60-120 minutes for treatment | Second |
| **GREEN** | Minor | Walking wounded; minor injuries; can help themselves | Lowest |
| **BLACK** | Deceased / Expectant | Dead or unsurvivable injury with current resources | None |

---

### 3.3 The START Algorithm — Step by Step

**INITIAL STEP — Walking test:**

Call out loudly: "Anyone who can walk, come over here!" Anyone who gets up and walks is tagged **GREEN**. Direct them to a designated assembly area away from the casualty treatment zone. They are ambulatory and can wait. Do not spend more time assessing them during triage.

Now proceed through non-ambulatory patients one by one.

---

**ASSESSMENT FOR EACH NON-AMBULATORY PATIENT:**

**Step 1: Respirations**

Open the airway using a head-tilt/chin-lift (if no spinal injury suspected) or jaw thrust (if spinal injury possible). Look, listen, and feel for breathing for no more than 10 seconds.

| Finding | Tag | Action |
|---------|-----|--------|
| Not breathing, airway remains closed | BLACK | Do not attempt resuscitation in MCI |
| Not breathing, airway opens with repositioning | RED | Proceed to assess perfusion |
| Breathing rate > 30 breaths/minute | RED | Proceed to assess perfusion |
| Breathing rate ≤ 30 breaths/minute | Continue → assess perfusion | |

**Step 2: Perfusion (Radial Pulse or Capillary Refill)**

Method A — Radial Pulse: Feel for a pulse at the wrist. Present = adequate perfusion. Absent = inadequate perfusion.

Method B — Capillary Refill (used when radial pulse assessment is difficult): Press firmly on the patient's fingernail or inner lip. Release. Count how many seconds until color returns to normal.

| Finding | Tag | Action |
|---------|-----|--------|
| No radial pulse (or capillary refill > 2 seconds) | RED | Proceed to assess mental status |
| Radial pulse present (or capillary refill ≤ 2 seconds) | Continue → assess mental status | |

If a patient is bleeding severely and you identify this during the perfusion check: stop and control the hemorrhage with direct pressure or tourniquet before proceeding. Uncontrolled hemorrhage is immediately life-threatening.

**Step 3: Mental Status**

Ask simple commands:
- "Squeeze my hand."
- "Open your eyes."
- "Tell me your name."

| Finding | Tag |
|---------|-----|
| Cannot follow simple commands | RED |
| Can follow simple commands | YELLOW |

---

### 3.4 Applying the START Tags

CERT teams use colored tags, colored ribbon, or colored tape to mark patients after assessment. If no manufactured tags are available, improvise with:
- Colored tape wrapped around the wrist or ankle
- Written markings on skin with a marker
- Pieces of colored cloth tied to the wrist

Mark tags clearly with:
- Category (color/letter)
- Time of assessment
- Any treatment provided (tourniquet applied, airway repositioned)
- Pulse and respiration rate if measured

**Tag placement:** Wrist or ankle — areas easily visible to treatment team members without moving the patient.

---

### 3.5 Reassessment

Patient condition changes. A patient tagged YELLOW who is left for 30 minutes may deteriorate to RED. CERT members or professional responders must reassess patients periodically:
- YELLOW patients: reassess every 10-15 minutes
- RED patients: continuously until treated or evacuated

Document every reassessment with time and findings.

## Chapter 4: Bleeding Control

### 4.1 The Threat of External Hemorrhage

Severe external bleeding is the most immediately preventable cause of death in trauma. An adult can lose approximately 5 liters of blood. Blood loss of 30% (1.5 liters) causes compensated shock. Loss of 40% (2 liters) causes decompensated shock — the body can no longer maintain blood pressure to vital organs. Death follows.

Controlling hemorrhage is therefore the highest priority treatment intervention after ensuring the airway.

---

### 4.2 Direct Pressure

Direct pressure is the first-line treatment for all external bleeding.

**Procedure:**
1. Apply a clean dressing (cloth, gauze, shirt, anything cleaner than the wound surface) directly over the wound.
2. Press firmly with both hands, or use your body weight if the wound is large.
3. Maintain constant, unrelenting pressure.
4. **Do not lift the dressing.** Lifting disrupts clot formation. If blood soaks through, place more material on top and press harder.
5. Hold for a minimum of 10 minutes without releasing pressure.
6. Once bleeding is controlled, bandage the dressing firmly in place.

**Wound packing (for deep wounds):**
For wounds that cannot be controlled with surface pressure alone (gunshot wounds, deep stab wounds, groin/armpit wounds where tourniquets cannot be applied):
1. Pack the wound tightly with gauze or clean cloth, pushing it firmly into the wound cavity.
2. Apply firm pressure over the packed wound.
3. Maintain pressure.

---

### 4.3 Pressure Bandage

A pressure bandage maintains direct pressure when the responder needs their hands for other tasks or when the patient must be transported.

**Improvised pressure bandage:**
1. Apply the primary dressing directly to the wound.
2. Place an additional bulky pad (rolled cloth) over the dressing.
3. Wrap bandage material (torn clothing strips, triangular bandages, roller gauze) in a spiral over the pad.
4. Tie or tape firmly — tight enough to maintain pressure, not so tight that circulation below is cut off.
5. Check for pulse below the bandage after application.

**Checking the bandage:**
- Too loose: Blood soaks through quickly; the wound edges separate.
- Too tight: Limb turns blue or purple below the bandage; no capillary refill; patient reports increasing pain or numbness.

---

### 4.4 Tourniquet Application

**When to apply a tourniquet:**
- Extremity (arm or leg) wound with severe bleeding that cannot be controlled with direct pressure alone.
- Amputation of a limb.
- Massive tissue loss from an extremity.
- Any situation where the responder cannot maintain direct pressure continuously.

**Tourniquet width:** A minimum of 4-5 cm (1.5-2 inches) wide. Narrower materials (rope, wire, cable tie) do not adequately distribute pressure over blood vessels and cause tissue damage without reliably stopping blood flow.

**Improvised tourniquet procedure:**
1. Select material: a torn strip of shirt, a web belt, a triangular bandage, a cravat. Fold to achieve the minimum width.
2. Position: 5-7 cm (2-3 inches) above the wound (between the wound and the heart), not over a joint.
3. Wrap twice around the limb.
4. Tie a square knot over a short sturdy stick, pen, or improvised windlass.
5. Twist the windlass until bleeding stops. Verify by checking the wound — it should stop, or become a slow ooze.
6. Secure the windlass with the remaining material so it cannot unwind.
7. **Write the time of application on the patient's skin** (forehead, upper arm, or next to the tourniquet) or on the triage tag. This is critical — medical personnel must know how long the tourniquet has been applied to assess tissue viability.
8. Do not cover the tourniquet. It must remain visible.
9. **Do not remove the tourniquet in the field.** Removal is a controlled medical procedure — removal in the field, without the ability to immediately control bleeding, can cause death.

**Manufactured tourniquets (CAT, SOFT-T, etc.):**
Follow the device-specific instructions. In general: apply above the wound, route the tail through the buckle, pull tight, close the windlass until bleeding stops, lock it, and note the time.

## Chapter 5: Shock

### 5.1 What is Shock?

Shock is the failure of the circulatory system to deliver adequate oxygen and nutrients to body tissues. At the cellular level, tissues are starving and dying. If untreated, shock is fatal.

In emergency/disaster contexts, the most common cause of shock is hemorrhagic shock (blood loss). Other causes include:
- Cardiac failure (pump failure)
- Severe allergic reaction (anaphylaxis — vasodilation causes blood to pool in peripheral vessels)
- Spinal cord injury (neurogenic shock — loss of vasomotor tone)
- Severe infection (septic shock — widespread inflammatory response)

---

### 5.2 Recognizing Shock

**Early signs (compensated shock):**
- Skin: pale, cool, clammy (diaphoretic)
- Pulse: rapid (heart rate increased to compensate for reduced stroke volume) and may feel weak or thready
- Breathing: rapid and shallow
- Anxiety, restlessness, or confusion
- Blood pressure may still be normal (the body is compensating)

**Late signs (decompensated shock):**
- Falling blood pressure
- Increasing confusion, then unconsciousness
- Very weak or absent pulse
- Breathing slow and irregular
- Lips and fingernails turning blue (cyanosis)

**Key principle:** Do not wait for blood pressure to fall before treating shock. By the time blood pressure drops, compensatory mechanisms have already failed. Treat for shock when early signs are present.

---

### 5.3 Treating Shock

**Step 1: Control the cause** — Stop any external bleeding (direct pressure, tourniquet). Remove the patient from ongoing threat.

**Step 2: Position** — Lay the patient flat. Elevate the legs 20-30 cm (8-12 inches) if there is no spinal or lower extremity injury and no breathing difficulty. This uses gravity to direct blood toward the core and vital organs.

Do NOT elevate legs if:
- Head, neck, or spinal injury is suspected
- Lower extremity fractures (elevating may worsen injury)
- Breathing is difficult (elevation compresses the diaphragm)
- Abdominal injury is suspected

**Step 3: Maintain temperature** — Cover the patient with blankets, jackets, or any available insulation. Hypothermia greatly worsens shock by impairing clotting and cardiac function (the lethal triad: hypothermia + acidosis + coagulopathy).

**Step 4: Airway and breathing** — Ensure the airway is open. Assist breathing if inadequate.

**Step 5: Do NOT give fluids by mouth** — A patient in shock may become unconscious and aspirate. Do not give food or water to a shock patient.

**Step 6: Psychological support** — Reassure the patient calmly and continuously. Anxiety and fear worsen shock by increasing metabolic demand. Speak in a calm, controlled voice. Tell the patient what you are doing and that help is coming.

## Chapter 6: Head, Neck, and Spinal Injuries

### 6.1 Mechanism of Injury

Spinal cord injury can occur without being obvious. Any mechanism that could have produced spinal injury must be treated as a suspected spinal injury until proven otherwise:
- Falls from height (even from standing if the patient landed on their head or back)
- Motor vehicle accidents
- Diving injuries (head striking the bottom)
- Direct blows to the head or back
- Any patient found unconscious whose mechanism of injury is unknown

**The danger:** The spine can be fractured without damaging the cord. Improper handling of such a patient can shift the fractured vertebrae, compressing or severing the cord, causing permanent paralysis or death.

---

### 6.2 Signs of Spinal Injury

- Pain or tenderness along the spine (neck or back)
- Numbness or tingling in arms or legs
- Weakness in arms or legs
- Patient reports inability to move or feel a limb
- Deformity along the spine visible or palpable
- Patient is unconscious following a mechanism that could involve spinal injury

**If any of these are present, or if the mechanism was consistent with spinal injury, treat as a spinal injury.**

---

### 6.3 Spinal Precautions in the Field

**Goal:** Keep the patient's head, neck, and back in a straight, neutral position (in-line) and prevent any flexion, extension, or rotation.

**Manual in-line stabilization:**
One responder kneels above the patient's head. Place both hands gently but firmly on either side of the head, with fingers pointing toward the patient's feet. Do NOT pull or apply traction — simply hold the head still in the position it was found (unless it is in an obviously impossible position, such as bent sharply to one side, in which case gently bring it to neutral only if the patient has no increase in pain or resistance).

Maintain manual stabilization throughout any treatment and until the patient can be secured on a spine board or backboard.

**Log roll:**
To turn a suspected spinal injury patient, use a minimum of 3 responders:
1. One at the head (leads the movement)
2. One at the torso
3. One at the legs/hips

On a count from the head-controller: roll the patient as a single rigid unit, like a log. The body must not bend or twist at any segment. This allows examination of the back and placement on a backboard.

**Improvised cervical collar:**
A cervical collar limits neck movement. Improvise from a rolled sleeping bag, foam pad, or thick folded cloth placed around the neck and taped or tied loosely in place. It should not be so tight that it compresses the airway.

---

### 6.4 Head Injury

**Signs of significant head injury:**
- Loss of consciousness (any duration)
- Confusion, disorientation, or amnesia
- Unequal pupils (one larger than the other)
- Clear fluid draining from ears or nose
- "Battle's sign" — bruising behind the ear
- Vomiting
- Seizures

**Field treatment of head injury:**
- Maintain airway
- Treat any external bleeding (scalp wounds bleed heavily but can be controlled with direct pressure)
- Assume spinal injury is present
- Do not give anything by mouth
- Position on the side (recovery position) if unconscious and no spinal injury concern, to prevent aspiration
- Monitor breathing continuously
- Keep the patient calm and still

**What NOT to do:**
- Do not apply pressure to a skull fracture — you may drive bone fragments into the brain
- Do not remove an impaled object — stabilize it in place
- Do not give pain medication that may alter mental status assessment

## Chapter 7: Splinting Fractures

### 7.1 Why Splint?

A fracture (broken bone) that is not immobilized causes:
- Severe pain with every movement
- Continued damage to surrounding soft tissue, blood vessels, and nerves from sharp bone edges
- Risk of compartment syndrome (pressure buildup in the muscle compartment)
- Interference with victim's ability to walk or use the limb

Splinting stabilizes the fracture, reduces pain, and allows the patient to be moved.

---

### 7.2 Before Splinting — Assess CSM

Before applying any splint, check and document:
- **C — Circulation:** Is there a pulse below the fracture? Feel at the wrist (radial pulse) for arm fractures, or at the foot (dorsalis pedis pulse) for leg fractures.
- **S — Sensation:** Can the patient feel you touching their fingers or toes below the fracture?
- **M — Movement:** Can the patient move their fingers or toes below the fracture?

Absent pulse, numbness, or inability to move indicates the bone is compressing a blood vessel or nerve. This is a medical emergency requiring immediate professional care.

After splinting, reassess CSM. If circulation was present before and absent after, the splint is too tight — loosen immediately.

---

### 7.3 Splinting Procedure

1. **Prepare materials:** Rigid component (boards, branches, tightly rolled magazines), padding (cloth, foam, leaves), and binding material (cloth strips, cravats, bandages).

2. **Pad the rigid component:** Never place rigid material against bare skin without padding. Pad generously — pressure sores develop within hours on unpadded bony prominences.

3. **Apply rigid component:** Position alongside the injured limb. The splint must extend from the joint above the fracture to the joint below it. For a mid-forearm fracture, the splint extends from the elbow to the palm.

4. **Secure with bindings:** Wrap bindings above the fracture site, below it, and over the fracture if the splint extends past it. Avoid placing bindings directly over the fracture.

5. **Check the tension:** You should be able to slide two fingers under the binding. Tighter restricts circulation; looser provides no immobilization.

6. **Elevate:** Elevate the splinted limb above heart level to reduce swelling (if no fracture of the pelvis or femur).

7. **Reassess CSM** after splinting.

**Specific considerations:**

*Finger/toe fracture (buddy taping):* Tape the injured digit to the adjacent healthy digit with padding between them.

*Forearm/wrist fracture:* Splint the palmar surface with padding, secure, and support in a sling with the elbow bent at 90 degrees.

*Ankle/foot fracture:* The "pillow splint" — wrap a pillow or sleeping bag U-shaped around the foot and ankle, and secure with bandages over the top. Maintains the foot in a neutral position.

*Collarbone (clavicle) fracture:* Use a figure-eight bandage (looped around both shoulders, crossing between the shoulder blades). Do not attempt to realign the bones. Sling the arm on the affected side.

## Chapter 8: Burns

### 8.1 Burn Classification

**First-degree (superficial) burns:**
Affect only the outer layer of skin (epidermis).
- Appearance: Red, dry, no blisters. Sunburn is a common example.
- Pain: Painful to touch.
- Treatment: Cool with cool (not cold) water for 10-20 minutes. Cover lightly. Do not apply ice, butter, oil, or toothpaste.
- Outcome: Heals in 3-5 days.

**Second-degree (partial thickness) burns:**
Affect the epidermis and part of the dermis.
- Appearance: Red, blistered, wet/shiny surface.
- Pain: Very painful.
- Treatment: Cool with cool water. Do NOT break blisters (intact blisters protect against infection). Cover with a clean, non-adhesive dressing. Do not use fluffy cotton that will stick to the wound.
- Outcome: Heals in 2-3 weeks without infection; may scar.

**Third-degree (full thickness) burns:**
Destroy the entire depth of the skin, including nerve endings and potentially underlying tissue.
- Appearance: White, brown, or black; dry and leathery; may appear waxy.
- Pain: Surprisingly little pain at the burn site (nerves are destroyed), but surrounding first and second-degree areas are painful.
- Treatment: Do NOT cool large third-degree burns — the patient is already at high risk of hypothermia. Cover with a dry, clean dressing. Treat for shock. This is a major medical emergency.
- Outcome: Requires skin grafting; permanent scarring without surgical treatment.

---

### 8.2 Estimating Burn Surface Area (Rule of Nines)

For planning treatment priorities and communicating with professional responders:

| Body Region | Percentage |
|-------------|-----------|
| Head and neck | 9% |
| Each arm | 9% |
| Chest (front) | 9% |
| Abdomen (front) | 9% |
| Upper back | 9% |
| Lower back | 9% |
| Each thigh | 9% |
| Each lower leg (below knee) | 9% |
| Perineum | 1% |

A burn covering the patient's palm (including fingers) equals approximately 1% of body surface area — useful for estimating smaller irregular burns.

**Critical size thresholds:**
- Burns >20% BSA (body surface area) in adults, >10% in children/elderly: High risk of hypovolemic shock.
- Third-degree burns >5% BSA: Major emergency.
- Burns involving hands, feet, face, genitalia, or major joints: Functional emergency even if small area.
- Burns of the face/airway: **Immediately life-threatening** — the airway will swell and close.

---

### 8.3 Special Burn Situations

**Inhalation injury:**
Suspect if: Burns around the face, singed nasal hairs, hoarse voice, soot in the mouth or throat, stridor (high-pitched breathing sounds), patient was in a closed burning space.

Airway burns are a true emergency. The airway can swell completely shut within minutes to hours. Signs of impending airway closure: increasing hoarseness, difficulty swallowing, stridor, respiratory distress.

Treatment: Position upright. High-flow oxygen if available. Urgent evacuation. Do not wait for full airway obstruction to develop.

**Chemical burns:**
Remove contaminated clothing (wear gloves if possible). Irrigate copiously with water for at least 20-30 minutes. For dry chemical burns, brush off the chemical first, then irrigate. Do not attempt to "neutralize" acid with base or vice versa — the chemical reaction generates heat and worsens the burn.

**Electrical burns:**
The entry and exit wounds may appear small while massive internal damage has occurred along the current path. Electrical burns carry a high risk of cardiac arrhythmia. Check pulse. Treat as a major burn regardless of wound appearance. Do not touch the patient until you have confirmed the power source is off.

## Chapter 9: Hypothermia and Hyperthermia in Disaster Contexts

(See also Chapter 9 of fm21-76-survival.md for detailed treatment protocols)

### 9.1 Disaster-Specific Considerations

Mass casualty events create hypothermia risk even in temperate conditions:
- Victims lying on cold ground
- Shock victims (peripheral vasoconstriction, reduced perfusion)
- Wet clothing from sprinkler systems, fire hose runoff, rain
- Prolonged extrication

**Prevention in the treatment area:**
- Insulate all patients from the ground (foam pads, cardboard, folded tarps — anything between patient and ground)
- Cover all patients who are not being actively treated
- Prioritize covering the head (significant heat loss from an uncovered head)
- Move patients out of wind and precipitation

### 9.2 Hyperthermia (Heat-Related Illness)

**Heat cramps:** Salt depletion. Move to shade, rest, oral hydration with electrolytes.

**Heat exhaustion:** Heavy sweating, cool pale skin, weakness, headache, nausea. Move to cool environment, remove clothing, cool with water, elevate legs, oral hydration.

**Heat stroke:** Hot skin (may or may not be sweating), mental status changes, high temperature (>40°C/104°F). This is a medical emergency. Aggressive cooling is the only treatment — immerse, wet and fan, apply ice to neck/armpits/groin. Immediate evacuation.

## Chapter 10: Airway Management

### 10.1 Airway Anatomy

Air enters through the nose and mouth, passes through the pharynx (throat), then the larynx (voice box, containing the epiglottis), then the trachea, and into the lungs. The most common airway obstruction in an unconscious patient is the tongue falling backward against the posterior pharynx, blocking airflow.

### 10.2 Manual Airway Opening Techniques

**Head-tilt/chin-lift (no spinal injury):**
Place one hand on the forehead and two fingers of the other hand under the chin (on the bony part, not the soft tissue). Tilt the head backward while lifting the chin upward. This extends the neck and lifts the tongue off the posterior pharynx.

**Jaw thrust (suspected spinal injury):**
Kneel behind the patient's head. Place your index fingers behind the angles of the jaw (just below the ear on each side). Apply upward pressure to move the jaw forward. This opens the airway without extending the neck.

---

### 10.3 Recovery Position

For an unconscious patient who is breathing adequately and has no suspected spinal injury, the recovery position (lateral recumbent position) prevents aspiration if the patient vomits.

**Procedure:**
1. Kneel beside the patient.
2. Place the arm nearest you extended outward at 90 degrees to the body, elbow bent, palm up.
3. Bring the far arm across the chest and hold the back of the hand against the patient's near cheek.
4. With your other hand, pull up the far knee so the foot is flat on the ground.
5. Keeping the patient's hand against their cheek, pull on the bent knee to roll the patient toward you onto their side.
6. Adjust the upper leg so the hip and knee are at 90 degrees (stabilizes the position).
7. Tilt the head back slightly to maintain airway.
8. Monitor breathing continuously.

---

### 10.4 Rescue Breathing Basics

**When to provide rescue breathing:**
- Patient is not breathing
- Patient has a pulse (if no pulse, CPR is required — compressions plus ventilations)
- In an MCI context, rescue breathing is generally not initiated for patients without a pulse during CERT operations (a BLACK tag is applied)

**Mouth-to-mouth:**
1. Ensure the airway is open (head-tilt/chin-lift).
2. Pinch the nose closed.
3. Create a seal over the patient's mouth with your mouth.
4. Give a breath lasting approximately 1 second — use enough volume to see the chest rise.
5. Remove your mouth and allow passive exhalation.
6. Repeat at 1 breath every 5-6 seconds (10-12 per minute for adults).

**Barrier devices:** A CPR face shield or pocket mask is strongly preferred to reduce infection risk. If neither is available and the responder is not trained or comfortable with mouth-to-mouth on a stranger, compression-only CPR is preferable to no action.

**When chest does not rise:**
- Reposition the head (head-tilt/chin-lift is insufficient or incorrect)
- Look in the mouth for visible obstruction (remove if visible, do not do blind finger sweeps)
- Ensure nose is pinched closed
- Ensure your seal is adequate

## Chapter 11: CERT Search and Rescue Basics

### 11.1 Light Search and Rescue

CERT teams are trained in "light" search and rescue — locating and extricating victims from lightly damaged structures or debris piles that do not require specialized heavy equipment. They do NOT perform:
- Confined space rescue (requires specialized equipment and training)
- Trench rescue
- Structural shoring or stabilization

**Rule of thumb:** If a victim requires heavy lifting equipment, specialized tools, or structural manipulation that could cause building collapse, wait for professional technical rescue teams. CERT assists; it does not replace.

---

### 11.2 The Search

**Systematic coverage:** A search team covers a building or area in a systematic pattern so no room or area is missed. Common pattern: search each floor top-to-bottom, systematically covering each room before moving to the next.

**Search entry assessment:**
Before entering each room or debris area:
- Check the door for heat (sign of fire on the other side)
- Check the floor for structural integrity before placing full weight
- Identify entry and exit routes in case conditions change

**Calling out:** Announce yourself as "CERT search team." Listen for any response — voice, tapping on walls or pipes, any noise. Victims who cannot speak may still make sounds. Listen carefully in all directions.

**Marking searched areas:** Mark the outside of searched rooms or buildings with a standardized system:
- Single diagonal line: entry into the search
- X (double diagonal): area fully searched
- Inside the X: number of victims found (living/dead)

---

### 11.3 The Extraction

Once a victim is located, the extraction method depends on their condition and the environment.

**Conscious, ambulatory victim:** Walk them out if the path is safe. Assist if needed.

**Unconscious or non-ambulatory victim without suspected spinal injury:**
- Carry using two-person carry, blanket drag, or improvised stretcher
- Prioritize speed over gentleness if the environment is hazardous

**Suspected spinal injury victim:**
- Do not move until you can perform a log-roll onto a backboard
- Minimum 3 rescuers for log-roll (4+ preferred)
- Secure to backboard before moving

**Improvised stretcher:**
- Two strong poles (branches) through the arms of two buttoned jackets
- Stretch between the poles
- Test with a team member's weight before loading a patient

**Victim handoff to medical team:**
When delivering a patient to the treatment area, provide:
- Patient's triage tag color
- Location found
- Mechanism of injury if known
- Any treatment provided (tourniquet time, airway intervention)
- Patient's current condition

Documentation follows the patient. Never separate a patient from their triage tag.

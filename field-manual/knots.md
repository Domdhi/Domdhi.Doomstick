# Practical Knots — Seamanship Reference

**Source:** US Navy Seamanship manuals (pre-1928 editions). Published before 1928, public domain per 17 U.S.C. and pre-1976 copyright law.

---

## Introduction

The following knots form the core practical set taught in US Navy seamanship instruction prior to 1928. They cover the essential needs: stopping a rope from running, joining two ropes, forming a fixed loop, and securing a line to a post, rail, or another rope under load.

A knot not practiced is a knot not known. Read these descriptions and diagrams, then practice with actual rope. The difference between reading about a bowline and being able to tie one in the dark with wet hands is the difference between a sailor and a landsman.

**Terminology used:**
- **Working end:** The free end of rope being used to tie the knot
- **Standing part:** The main length of rope under tension
- **Bight:** A curved section of rope, not crossing itself
- **Loop:** A curved section of rope that crosses itself
- **Turn:** The rope going once around an object
- **Round turn:** The rope going once and a half around (one full circumference plus the approach)

## Knot 1: Overhand Knot (Thumb Knot)

**Use case:** The simplest stopper knot. Prevents a rope end from running through a hole, block, or ring. The foundation of many more complex knots.

**Strength characteristics:** Reduces rope strength by approximately 50% at the knot. Jams tightly under load and can be difficult to untie after heavy use.

**When to use:** Preventing a rope end from running free; as the base for other knots. Not used where the knot must be easily untied after loading.

---

**Step-by-step:**

```
Step 1: Make a loop
    
    |
    |
    working end crosses
    over the standing part
    |
    ------>
    (working end)
    
    Form a loop:
    
        ___
       /   \
      |     |
       \   /
        ---
        | <- working end passes
           behind the loop
```

```
Step 2: Pass the end through the loop

        ___
       /   \
      |     |
       \   /
        ---
         \
          -> working end
             threads up
             through the loop
             from below
```

```
Step 3: Draw tight

        ___
       / O \    <- the knot closes
      |     |      into a small lump
       \___/
         |
    standing part
```

**How to untie:** When not heavily loaded, push the knot body to loosen the loop, then withdraw the working end. After heavy loading, use a spike or marlinspike to pick the knot open.

## Knot 2: Figure-Eight Knot

**Use case:** A superior stopper knot. Larger than the overhand knot, so it stops in a larger hole or block. Retains better rope strength. The preferred stopper in rigging work. Also used as the foundation for the figure-eight follow-through loop (a climbing knot not described here, but built on this base).

**Strength characteristics:** Reduces rope strength by approximately 30-35% — significantly better than the overhand. Can be untied even after loading in most cases.

**When to use:** Whenever a stopper knot is needed and you have room for the larger bulk. Always preferred over the overhand for critical applications.

---

**Step-by-step:**

```
Step 1: Make a loop, working end goes OVER

          |
          | (standing part)
          |
          /-----\
         /       \
working end        \
crosses OVER        |
standing part       |
```

```
Step 2: Bring working end around BEHIND standing part

          |
          | (standing part)
          |          
          /-----\
         /       |
        |    working end
        |    goes BEHIND
         \   the standing part
```

```
Step 3: Pass working end through the original loop FROM THE FRONT

          |
          |
          |
          /-----\
working  /       |
end  -> |   goes through
  (up   |   the loop
  from   \
  below)  ----
```

```
Step 4: Draw tight — the finished knot looks like a figure 8

           |
          /|\
         / | \
        /  |  \
       | upper |
       |  loop |
       \  | /
        \ |/
         \|
         /|\
        / | \
       | lower|
       |  loop|
        \_|_/
           |
```

**How to untie:** Easier than the overhand knot after loading. Push the two loops of the "8" toward each other, which loosens the structure. Withdraw the working end.

## Knot 3: Square Knot (Reef Knot)

**Use case:** Joining two ropes of the same diameter for a flat, secure connection. Historically used to reef (reduce area of) sails — hence "reef knot." Used for bandages, ties, and any application where two rope ends of equal size must be joined.

**Strength characteristics:** Reduces rope strength by approximately 40-45%. The crucial limitation: the square knot is NOT a reliable bend (rope-joining knot) for critical loads. It can capsize (flip inside out) when one end is pulled sideways, releasing both ends. Use a sheet bend or double fisherman's for critical loads.

**The mnemonic:** "Right over left, then left over right." If you tie it the opposite sequence both times (right over left, right over left), you produce a granny knot — which jams, does not lie flat, and is structurally inferior.

---

**Step-by-step:**

```
Step 1: Cross the right end over the left (right over left)
        and pass it underneath

    LEFT end        RIGHT end
       |                |
        \              /
         \            /
          ----      ----
              \    /
               ----
          RIGHT crosses over LEFT,
          then comes up underneath
```

```
Step 2: Cross the left end over the right (left over right)
        and pass through

    (what was right      (what was left
     is now going        is now going
     left)               right)
         |                  |
          \                /
           ---          ---
               \      /
                \    /
                 ----
          LEFT crosses over RIGHT,
          then threads through
```

```
Step 3: The finished square knot — flat and symmetric

    --------     --------
    |       \   /       |
    |        ---        |
    |       /   \       |
    --------     --------
    
    Both sides mirror each other.
    Both ends exit on the same side as
    the loop on their respective side.
    If they exit on opposite sides: granny knot.
```

**How to untie:** Pull both ends sideways (away from each other across the knot). The knot releases readily when not jammed.

## Knot 4: Sheet Bend (Weaver's Knot)

**Use case:** Joining two ropes of DIFFERENT diameters. The sheet bend holds reliably where the square knot would slip or capsize. In sailing, used to attach sheets (control lines) to sails. Also called "weaver's knot" from its use in weaving to join threads.

**Strength characteristics:** Superior to the square knot for two-rope joins, especially with unequal diameters. For critical loads or wet rope, use the double sheet bend (make a second crossing of the small-diameter rope around the bight of the large rope).

**When to use:** Whenever joining two ropes of different sizes. Preferred over the square knot even when sizes are equal in critical applications.

---

**Step-by-step:**

```
Step 1: Form a bight (U-shape) in the thicker rope

    Thicker rope
    
          /--------\
         |          |
         |  bight   |
          \________/
    
    The bight is the bend — the rope
    does NOT cross itself here.
```

```
Step 2: Thread the thinner rope UP through the bight
        (entering from the bottom)

    Thicker rope (bight)
    
          /--------\
         |          |
         |          |
          \________/
               ^
               |
           Thinner rope
           comes up through
           the bight from below
```

```
Step 3: Pass the thinner rope UNDER both legs
        of the bight

    Thicker rope (bight)
         ====         ====
        /    \       /    \
       |      |     |      |
       |      |_____|      |
        \    /       \    /
         ====         ====
              -------
             /   <- thinner rope goes
            /       under both legs
```

```
Step 4: Bring the thinner rope back over itself
        (tuck it under its own standing part)

    Correct lock:
    Thinner rope's working end passes under
    its OWN standing part, not back through
    the bight. This locks the knot.
    
    The working end and standing part of the
    thinner rope exit on the SAME SIDE of
    the bight (not opposite sides).
```

**Double sheet bend:** For extra security or wet/slippery rope, make a second wrap of the thinner rope around both legs of the bight before tucking. Both wraps tuck under the same point.

**How to untie:** With moderate loads, slide the thinner rope off the bight of the thicker rope. Under heavy loads, push the bight toward the thinner rope wraps.

## Knot 5: Bowline — "The King of Knots"

**Use case:** Creates a fixed, non-slipping loop at the end of a rope. The loop will not tighten under load and will not loosen accidentally. Used for: lifelines, attaching a line to a fixed point (ring, bollard), making a loop to attach to another line, rescue applications (the loop can go around a person's chest).

**Strength characteristics:** Reduces rope strength by approximately 35-40%. Maintains a predictable loop size regardless of load. Can be untied after heavy loading (a major advantage over the figure-eight loop in some applications).

**The mnemonic:** "The rabbit comes out of its hole, runs around the tree, and goes back down the hole." The "hole" is the small loop (eye), the "tree" is the standing part, the "rabbit" is the working end.

---

**Step-by-step:**

```
Step 1: Form a small loop (the "hole") in the standing part.
        Working end is below, crossing UNDER the standing part.

    standing part
         |
         |
         o  <- small loop (eye)
        / \    working end
       /   \   is below here
      /     \
     working end
```

```
Step 2: "Rabbit comes out of the hole"
        Pass the working end UP through the small loop
        from below

    standing part
         |
         |
         o  <- small loop
        /|\
       / | \
      /  ^  \
         |
    working end
    comes UP through
    the loop (from below)
```

```
Step 3: "Around the tree"
        Pass the working end BEHIND the standing part

    standing part
         |
         |  <-- working end goes
         |      BEHIND this
         |
         o
        / \
       /   \
      working end exits
      left, goes behind
      the standing part,
      comes around to the front
```

```
Step 4: "Back down the hole"
        Pass the working end DOWN through the small loop
        (back the way it came — from above)

    standing part
         |
         |
         o  <- small loop
        /|\
       / | \
      /  v  \
         |
    working end
    passes DOWN through
    the loop (from above)
    — the reverse of Step 2
```

```
Step 5: Draw tight — check the result

    standing part
         |
         |
    _____|_____
   |           |
   |  the big  |  <- this is the fixed loop
   |  fixed    |     (its size = Step 1 loop size)
   |  loop     |
   |___________|
         |
         working end
         (should be INSIDE
          the fixed loop,
          pointing the same
          direction as the
          standing part)
```

**Verify the bowline:** The working end should lie inside the large loop, parallel to and next to the standing part. If the working end is outside the large loop, the knot is wrong.

**How to untie:** Slide the small loop over the back of the working end (the part that runs behind the standing part). Even after very heavy loading, the bowline can usually be broken free by bending the back of the knot sharply.

## Knot 6: Clove Hitch

**Use case:** Quick, adjustable attachment of a line to a post, rail, spar, or ring. Widely used in seamanship for making fast to cleats, bollards, and fenders. Also used in lashing operations.

**Strength characteristics:** Holds well when load is approximately perpendicular to the post. Can slip when load direction varies. Not appropriate as a primary fastening under heavy, shifting load — use a round turn and two half hitches for permanence.

**When to use:** Quick ties to posts or rails; starting and ending lashings; attaching fenders to a dock line.

---

**Step-by-step (on a post or rail):**

```
Step 1: Pass the rope over the post from the front.
        The working end hangs behind.

    [POST]
      |
    --+--  <- rope crosses over the post
      |
      v   working end behind
```

```
Step 2: Bring the working end around in front of the
        post again (crossing OVER the first wrap).
        Pass behind the post again.

    [POST]
      |
    --+--  first wrap
      |
    --+--  working end crosses
      ^    OVER (not under) the
      |    first wrap; then goes
      behind the post
```

```
Step 3: Tuck the working end under the second wrap
        (the one just made), then pull tight.

    [POST]
    
       second wrap
       ____
      |    |
      |    |  <- working end tucks
      |____|     under the second wrap
      ^
      first wrap
```

**Alternatively (tying in hand, then dropping over a post):**

```
Step 1: Make a loop, working end on top.

Step 2: Make a second loop identical to the first
        alongside it, working end on top again.

Step 3: Place the second loop on top of the first.

Step 4: Drop both loops over the post.
        Tighten.
```

**How to untie:** Slide the clove hitch sideways along the post (if possible) to loosen, then unwrap.

## Knot 7: Half Hitch and Two Half Hitches

**Use case:** A half hitch alone is not secure — it requires a second hitch to become reliable. Two half hitches create a secure, adjustable attachment to a ring, post, or rail. Used as the finishing tie after a round turn, or as a standalone hitch for moderate loads.

**Strength characteristics:** Two half hitches are more reliable than a clove hitch when load direction varies. Easily adjusted and released.

---

**Half hitch:**

```
Pass the working end around the post or through the ring.
Bring the working end over the standing part
and through the resulting loop.

     |  (object)
     |
   --+    <- working end passes over
     |       standing part, through loop
     ^
     one half hitch
```

**Two half hitches:**

```
Repeat the half hitch in the same direction
as the first.

     |  (object)
     |
   --+    <- first half hitch
   --+    <- second half hitch
       <- working end exits
```

**Round turn and two half hitches:** Pass the rope once fully around the post (making a complete round turn), then add two half hitches on the standing part. The round turn takes the immediate load while you make the hitches — very useful for controlling heavy loads.

**How to untie:** Slip the hitches backward off the standing part.

## Knot 8: Taut-Line Hitch (Rolling Hitch on own rope)

**Use case:** Creates an adjustable loop that slides when not under load but grips when tensioned. Classic use: tent and tarp guy lines — the hitch slides to adjust tension, then locks when the line is loaded. Also used for any line requiring length adjustment without re-tying.

**Strength characteristics:** Grips well on its own standing part when load is axial (in line with the rope). Will slip if the load is perpendicular to the rope.

---

**Step-by-step:**

```
Step 1: Form a loop by crossing the working end
        over the standing part (going away from tent/anchor).

    tent/anchor
       |
       | (standing part, going toward anchor)
       |
       X  <- working end crosses over
       |
    working end
```

```
Step 2: Make TWO wraps of the working end around
        the standing part, INSIDE the loop
        (working toward the tent/load end)

    tent/anchor
       |
       =  <- 2nd wrap (working toward load)
       =  <- 1st wrap
       X  <- where working end crossed over
       |
       |
    (direction of load)
```

```
Step 3: Bring the working end back and make ONE MORE
        wrap OUTSIDE the original cross point
        (on the tent/load side of the two wraps)

    tent/anchor
       |
       =  <- final wrap (outside/load side of X)
       =  <- 2nd wrap  |
       =  <- 1st wrap  | these two are
       X               | on the anchor side
       |
       |
    working end exits toward anchor
```

```
Step 4: Verify: Three wraps total — two on the anchor side
        of the crossing point, one on the load side.
        
        This asymmetry is what creates the cam action:
        - Push toward load: hitch slides freely
        - Load tension: hitch grips the rope
```

**How to adjust:** Grasp the knot body and push along the rope toward the load to loosen; let the load pull to tighten and lock.

**How to untie:** Remove the load, push knot to slack the line, unwrap.

---

## Knot 9: Rolling Hitch (Grip on a Rope or Pole Under Load)

**Use case:** Grips another rope or pole parallel to the load. Used to: attach a secondary line to a loaded rope, take the strain from one line to another, attach a line to a rail or spar. The rolling hitch will not slip along the object when loaded in the direction away from the double wrap.

**Strength characteristics:** Grips very effectively on rope or smooth poles when loaded axially in the correct direction. Does not hold if loaded in the direction of the single wrap.

---

**Step-by-step (on a horizontal rope or spar):**

```
Identify which direction the load will come from.
The double wrap goes on the load side.

    load direction -->
    
    [spar or rope being gripped]
    
Step 1: Pass the working end around the spar.
        Cross OVER the standing part (on the load side).
        Make a second wrap around the spar (still on load side).
        
    --- rope crosses over standing part here
    --- second wrap (same side as load direction)
```

```
Step 2: Make a third wrap on the OTHER side of the
        two wraps (the non-load side).
        Tuck the working end under this final wrap.

    load side ->     non-load side
    
    =   first wrap
    =   second wrap   =  <- final wrap
                         <- working end tucked under
```

```
Step 3: Tighten by pulling on the working end.
        Load in the direction of the double wraps:
        the hitch grips and does not slip.
        
    [spar]
    == <- double wrap, load direction
    =  <- single wrap, opposite side
       <- working end
```

**Memory aid:** Two wraps on the pulling side; one wrap on the other side. Load toward the two wraps.

**How to untie:** Remove the load, unwrap in reverse.

## Knot 10: Fisherman's Knot (True Fisherman's, Water Knot)

**Use case:** Joining two fishing lines or thin, slippery cords end-to-end. Also called the "angler's knot" or "water knot." Creates a strong, compact join that passes through line guides on a fishing pole. Can also join the ends of webbing or flat material (in its tape/ring-bend variant).

**Strength characteristics:** Reduces line strength by approximately 35-40%. More compact than other bends, passes through guides well. Both overhand knots can be made into figure-eight knots for additional security (double fisherman's or grapevine knot — used in climbing for joining rappel ropes).

---

**Step-by-step:**

```
Tie two overhand knots — one on each rope,
with each rope threaded through the other's loop.

Step 1: Hold the two rope ends parallel,
        overlapping by about 20-25 cm.

    ====|===]      Working end A -->
              
    <-- Working end B    [===|====
    
    (Overlapping section, 20-25 cm)
```

```
Step 2: Tie an overhand knot in rope A,
        incorporating rope B through the loop.

    Rope A forms a loop; rope B passes through it.
    
    Rope A: ___________
                ___
               /   \
              | A's |  <- rope B passes through here
              | loop|
               \___/
                |
    Rope B: ___________|
```

```
Step 3: Tie an overhand knot in rope B in the
        SAME WAY, incorporating rope A.
        The two overhand knots are tied in
        opposite ends of the overlapping section.

    Rope A's overhand knot ---v
    
    ====|---(A's knot)---(B's knot)---|====
         ^--- Rope B's overhand knot
```

```
Step 4: Pull both standing parts to bring the
        two overhand knots together.
        They slide toward each other until they jam.

    Before:
    ======[(A knot)]---[(B knot)]======
    
    After (tightened):
    ======[(A knot)(B knot)]======
           ^---- compact double knot
```

**Double fisherman's (more secure):** Use figure-eight knots instead of overhand knots in Steps 2 and 3. The result is bulkier but significantly stronger and more secure for critical applications (rappelling, etc.).

**How to untie:** The fisherman's knot is notoriously difficult to untie after heavy loading. Push the two individual overhand knots away from each other (back toward their respective rope ends) to create slack, then untie each individual overhand knot. Soaking in water before untying can help on wet rope.

## Quick Reference: Knot Selection

| Need | Best knot | Backup |
|------|-----------|--------|
| Stop rope from running through block/hole | Figure-eight | Overhand |
| Join two ropes, same diameter | Square knot (light) / Sheet bend (heavy) | Sheet bend |
| Join two ropes, different diameter | Sheet bend | Double sheet bend |
| Join two fishing lines or thin cord | Fisherman's knot | Double fisherman's |
| Fixed loop at rope end | Bowline | — |
| Quick attachment to post or rail | Clove hitch | Round turn + two half hitches |
| Secure attachment to post or rail | Round turn + two half hitches | — |
| Adjustable loop on own rope | Taut-line hitch | — |
| Grip on another rope under load | Rolling hitch | — |

## Common Mistakes

**Square knot errors:**
- Granny knot: both overhand crossings in the same direction. Lies at an angle; jams; is not a reef knot.
- Check: Both ends should lie flat and parallel on their side of the knot.

**Bowline errors:**
- Working end exits outside the large loop instead of inside.
- Working end runs parallel to and outside the standing part (instead of inside).
- Check: Working end must lie inside the fixed loop, alongside the standing part.

**Sheet bend errors:**
- Working end and standing part of the thin rope exit on opposite sides of the bight (instead of same side).
- This is a common error and produces a knot that looks right but slips.
- Check: Both ends of the thinner rope exit from the same side of the thicker rope's bight.

**Clove hitch errors:**
- Second crossing goes under the first wrap instead of over it.
- This produces a less secure hitch.
- Check: The crossing must go over the first wrap.

**Taut-line hitch errors:**
- Only two total wraps instead of three (one side, one side, one side) — the knot will slip.
- Check: You must have three total wraps — two on one side, one on the other.

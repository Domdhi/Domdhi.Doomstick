**Source:** US Army Field Manual FM 21-76 (1992). US Government work, public domain per 17 U.S.C. § 105.

## Chapter 1: Core Survival Principles — The S.U.R.V.I.V.A.L. Acronym

The US Army teaches survival through a structured acronym: **S.U.R.V.I.V.A.L.** Each letter represents a key principle or action.

### S — Size Up the Situation

Before taking any action, assess three things:

**Surroundings:** Stop moving. Listen. Look around carefully. Identify threats: enemy activity, dangerous terrain, weather, wildlife. Determine what resources are immediately available (water, shelter materials, food).

**Physical condition:** Inventory your injuries. Are you bleeding? In shock? Dehydrated? Cold? Assess your gear. What do you have and what is missing?

**Equipment:** Take stock of everything in your pockets, pack, and on your person. A seemingly useless item can be critical. A button can be a fish hook. Parachute cord can rig a shelter. Know what you have before you decide what you need.

### U — Use All Your Senses, Undue Haste Makes Waste

Rushing causes errors. A hasty decision to move may lead you deeper into danger. Slow down. Think before you act.

**Use all five senses:** Smell can alert you to water (vegetation, mud) or danger (smoke, chemicals, animal musk). Sound can locate water or indicate human activity. Touch tells you temperature and material properties. Taste (with caution) helps identify plant edibility. Sight obviously dominates, but it can fool you — especially at night or in dense vegetation.

**Think through consequences:** Every action has a result. Moving from cover exposes you. Building a fire signals your position. Eating an unknown plant risks poisoning. Think through the likely outcomes before acting.

### R — Remember Where You Are

**Navigation awareness:** Always know your general location. Before the emergency, you should have noted terrain features, compass headings, and landmarks. In the aftermath, orient yourself:

- Look for prominent terrain features visible from your position (ridgelines, rivers, peaks).
- Determine the direction of north using sun or stars.
- Estimate how far you have traveled from your last known point.
- Identify potential rescue corridors (roads, rivers, open ground visible from the air).

**Mark your position:** If you establish a camp, mark it so you can return to it and so rescuers can find it. Create ground-to-air signals. Do not wander aimlessly.

### V — Vanquish Fear and Panic

Fear is the mind-killer in a survival situation. It causes rapid, shallow breathing, poor decision-making, tunnel vision, and physical freezing. Panic is fear that has overwhelmed rational thought.

**Control fear through action:** The act of doing something purposeful — making a fire, building shelter, organizing your pack — occupies the mind and reduces panic. When you feel panic rising, stop. Breathe slowly and deeply. Count to ten. Then focus on the next single task.

**Accept your situation:** Denial wastes energy. Accept that you are in a survival situation. Accept that conditions may be harsh. This emotional acceptance frees you to act constructively rather than waiting for conditions to improve on their own.

**Confidence from competence:** The more survival skills you have practiced before the emergency, the less fear you will experience during it. Practice these skills routinely so they are automatic under stress.

### I — Improvise

Military survival training emphasizes improvisation over dependence on specific equipment. Every object can serve multiple purposes:

- A soda can cut open makes a pot, a reflector, or fish hooks.
- A shirt can be a water filter, a tourniquet, a signal panel, or rope.
- A mirror shard can signal aircraft.
- A shoelace is cordage for trapping, fire-making bows, or lashing.
- A rock can be a hammer, a scraper, a cutting tool, or fire-making percussion.

**Study your environment:** Native materials can substitute for almost any piece of gear. Bamboo makes excellent containers. Birch bark makes excellent fire tinder and waterproofing. Cattail down is insulation. Pine resin seals containers. Animal fat greases tools and repels insects.

### V — Value Living

The will to survive is the most critical survival asset. Soldiers who have survived extreme conditions consistently report that the decision to live — the refusal to give up — was the difference between life and death.

**Reasons to survive:** Think of the people who need you. Your family, your unit, your responsibilities. These concrete motivations sustain the will to continue when conditions are at their worst.

**Manage your mental state:** Survival situations can last days or weeks. Establish routines. Maintain your appearance as much as possible — it reinforces your identity and self-respect. Set small, achievable goals. Celebrate each success (a fire lit, water procured, shelter built). This prevents the psychological collapse that kills people who have adequate physical resources.

**Avoid the survivor's greatest enemy — despair:** Despair comes from focusing on the total scale of the problem. Counter it by focusing only on the next immediate task. You do not need to be rescued today. You need to make it to tonight. Then tomorrow.

### A — Act Like the Natives

Observe how native animals and plants adapt to the local environment. They have solved the same survival problems over millennia.

**Animals as water indicators:** Most animals drink regularly. Animal trails converging in a valley bottom often lead to water. Birds flock toward water in the evening. Insects congregate near water.

**Plants as food and material indicators:** Where edible plants grow is where water and nutrients exist. Plants that humans have long used for food in a region are your first targets. A forested area with a stream usually provides sufficient resources.

**Weather behavior:** Native animals respond to weather changes before humans perceive them. Swallows flying low indicate an approaching storm (insects drop in altitude before rain). Cattle lying down in herds indicate rain. Ant columns moving in covered routes indicate incoming precipitation.

### L — Live by Your Wits, But for Now Learn Basic Skills

Knowledge is weightless and cannot be lost, stolen, or broken. The skills you learn before an emergency are the ones you will have when you need them.

Basic skills that must be mastered:
- Building a fire in any weather condition
- Constructing a weatherproof shelter from natural materials
- Finding and purifying water
- Navigating without instruments
- Identifying safe food sources
- Signaling for rescue

These skills do not come from reading alone. Practice them. Build a fire in your backyard. Construct a debris shelter on a camping trip. Navigate by stars. The difference between reading about it and having done it is the difference between survival and death.

## Chapter 2: Priority of Survival Needs

The US Army establishes a clear priority order for survival actions. Violating this order kills people who have sufficient resources to survive.

### The Survival Priority Sequence

**1. Protection from immediate threats**
Immediate threats must be addressed first regardless of other needs. If you are in a combat zone, take cover. If you are in a flash flood, get to high ground. If you are in a building fire, evacuate. If you are in sub-zero temperature with wet clothing, your first action is getting dry and warm. Every minute of exposure to hypothermia reduces your decision-making capacity and survival window.

**2. Signaling**
If rescue is possible, signal early and continuously. Many survivors are found within 24-72 hours if they signal effectively. People who assume they will self-rescue and delay signaling have died within sight of rescue teams. If you are in a survival situation where rescue is possible, establish a signal fire, lay out ground signals, and carry a signal mirror before you focus on long-term food and shelter.

**3. Water**
A healthy adult can survive 3 days without water in moderate conditions. In hot weather with exertion, this drops to hours. Dehydration impairs judgment before physical symptoms become obvious. Secure water early.

**4. Shelter**
In cold or wet conditions, shelter may supersede water in urgency. Hypothermia kills in minutes to hours. A person with adequate shelter can survive days without food and water. In hot, dry conditions, shelter (shade) reduces water loss. In all conditions, shelter enables rest, which is necessary to maintain cognitive function.

**5. Fire**
Fire provides warmth, water purification, cooking, signal capability, and psychological comfort. In cold climates it is life-critical. In warm climates it is still important for purifying water, cooking food, and maintaining morale. Fire also provides protection from insects and predators.

**6. Food**
A healthy adult can survive 3 weeks without food. Food is the least urgent of the primary survival needs. However, food becomes important in extended survival situations because it provides energy for maintaining other priorities (gathering wood, carrying water, building shelter) and is critical for maintaining body temperature in cold environments.

### Why This Order Matters

Survivors have died of dehydration while building elaborate shelters. They have died of hypothermia while searching for food. They have died of exposure in the shadow of a signal fire they never lit. The priority order exists because the human body has different timelines of failure for each threat. Assess each threat, assign it to its proper place in the sequence, and address them in order.

## Chapter 3: Shelter Construction

Shelter controls your microenvironment. In survival situations, you are not trying to be comfortable — you are trying to control heat loss or gain.

### General Shelter Principles

**Site selection criteria:**
- Above the valley bottom (cold air pools in low ground; morning dew is heavier; flooding risk is higher)
- On level or gently sloping ground (not directly on a hilltop exposed to wind)
- Within 100 meters of a water source, but not in the flood zone
- Near natural windbreaks (rock outcroppings, dense vegetation)
- Not under dead trees or branches (widow-makers fall in wind)
- Not in drainage channels (flash floods)
- Facing south in the northern hemisphere (maximizes solar gain)

**Minimum shelter requirements:**
- Block wind
- Provide insulation between you and the ground (ground conducts heat away from the body faster than air)
- Keep rain off you

**The ground is your enemy:** Wet, cold ground pulls heat from your body at a rate far exceeding air convection. Always put insulation between yourself and the ground: leaves, pine boughs, bark, grass, anything that breaks the conduction path.

---

### 3.1 Lean-To Shelter

The lean-to is the simplest shelter. It is fast to construct, requires no tools, and provides good rain protection and heat reflection from a fire.

**Construction:**

1. Find two trees approximately 2-3 meters apart (about shoulder width plus arm's length on each side).

2. Lash a horizontal ridgepole between the trees at chest height. Use paracord, bark strips, or vines. This is your structural spine.

3. Lean branches, sticks, or poles from the ridgepole to the ground at approximately a 45-degree angle. These should be spaced 15-20 cm apart. This forms the sloped roof structure.

4. Layer overlapping material over the poles starting from the bottom: pine boughs (with needles pointing down), bark slabs, large leaves, grass. Work from the bottom up like shingles so each layer sheds water over the layer below.

5. Cover the sides with additional branches and insulating material.

6. Build your fire 1-1.5 meters in front of the open face. Position a reflector wall (large stacked logs or rocks) on the far side of the fire to bounce heat toward the shelter.

**Improvement — triple-lean-to:** Build two lean-tos facing each other with a narrow gap between them, leaving a door opening at one end. Roof over the ridge. This is essentially a debris hut variant that provides 360-degree protection.

**Advantages:** Fast (1-2 hours), does not require enclosed space, works well with fire heating. **Disadvantages:** Less efficient heat retention than enclosed shelters; wind from the open face is a problem.

---

### 3.2 Debris Hut

The debris hut is the premier single-person survival shelter in temperate forests. It is fully enclosed, provides excellent insulation, and can keep a person alive in near-freezing temperatures with no fire at all — body heat alone is sufficient if the insulation is adequate.

**How it works:** A debris hut is functionally a sleeping bag made of natural materials. It traps your body heat in a layer of dead air insulation (dead leaves, pine needles, dry grass) that surrounds you. The smaller the interior space, the faster it heats up. You should only just be able to roll over inside.

**Construction:**

**Step 1: Find or make the ridge pole.**
Select a pole 3-4 meters long, straight, sturdy, roughly the diameter of your wrist. Prop one end on a stump, rock, or forked branch about 60-90 cm off the ground. The other end rests on the ground.

**Step 2: Create the ribbed frame.**
Lean branches against both sides of the ridgepole, angled about 45 degrees. These ribs support the debris without collapsing inward. Space them 30-45 cm apart. Fill gaps with smaller sticks.

**Step 3: Pile on debris.**
Begin piling leaves, dry grass, pine needles, bracken fern — any dry, lightweight, fluffy material — over the frame. Start at the bottom and work up (shingle method). You need a MINIMUM of 60 cm (2 feet) of debris over every surface, and more is better. At 60 cm, the shelter will keep you warm to just above freezing. At 90 cm (3 feet), it will keep you warm in temperatures below freezing. Use your arm as a measuring stick — debris depth should reach your elbow when you push down into it.

**Step 4: Stuff the interior.**
Fill the inside completely with dry insulating debris — the same materials used outside. Dry leaves, grass, anything that traps air. This is the material you will sleep in. Crawl in feet-first and burrow into the debris like a sleeping bag.

**Step 5: Door plug.**
Gather a large pile of debris and place it just inside the entrance. After crawling in, pull it in behind you to plug the door. A stuffed bag or armful of leaves works well.

**Testing your shelter:** Build it in the afternoon. Enter before dark. If you are cold after 20 minutes, the walls are not thick enough or your interior debris is insufficient. Rebuild with more material.

**Common mistakes:**
- Making it too large. The interior should fit your body with just enough room to roll over.
- Insufficient debris. When people say "enough," they usually have a third of what they need.
- Leaving air gaps in the frame that allow debris to fall through.
- Building directly on bare ground without a deep bed of insulating debris underneath.

---

### 3.3 Below-Ground Shelter (Trench Shelter)

In open terrain without forest resources — desert, plains, tundra — a below-ground trench shelter provides insulation and wind protection without requiring building materials.

**Construction in soil:**

1. Dig a trench 60 cm wider than your body and 30 cm deeper than your body when lying down.
2. If you have a poncho, tarp, or space blanket: stretch it across the trench and weigh down the edges with rocks or soil.
3. If no materials: lay the straightest branches you can find across the top of the trench, then pile soil over them until you have 30 cm of earth cover.
4. Leave a small ventilation hole at one end.
5. Line the interior with insulating material.

**In snow (snow trench):**

Snow is an excellent insulator. Air temperature inside a well-constructed snow shelter will stabilize around -1 to -5°C (30-23°F) regardless of outside temperature, as long as you have body heat to contribute.

1. Find a deep snowdrift (1 meter minimum).
2. Probe to find a solid section. Dig a horizontal tunnel into the drift, angling slightly upward so cold air pools at the entrance, not in the sleeping area.
3. Make the sleeping chamber just large enough for your body. Shape the ceiling into an arch (flat ceilings collapse; arched ceilings are structurally self-supporting).
4. Poke a ventilation hole through the ceiling with a stick. Mark the outside of the hole with a branch so you can find it and keep it clear.
5. Insulate the sleeping surface. Never sleep directly on snow.
6. Test for adequate ventilation: light a candle. If the flame flickers strongly, air movement is good. If it barely moves, add more ventilation.

**Warning signs of carbon monoxide buildup inside snow shelters:** headache, drowsiness, dizziness. If anyone in the shelter experiences these, open ventilation immediately. Never use a stove inside a snow shelter without verified ventilation.

## Chapter 4: Fire Making

Fire is the most psychologically powerful survival skill. It provides warmth, signals rescuers, purifies water, cooks food, repels insects, and gives a focal point for camp activity. An experienced survivor with reliable fire-starting knowledge can survive in conditions that would kill an equally fit but fire-ignorant person.

### 4.1 What You Need for Fire

**The fire triangle requires three elements:** Heat, fuel, and oxygen. Remove any one and fire dies.

**Tinder:** The smallest, driest, most easily ignited material. Tinder must catch a spark or small flame and sustain combustion long enough to ignite kindling.

Excellent tinder sources:
- Dry grass, twisted into a bundle (birds' nest shape works best)
- Birch bark (papery inner bark, not outer — contains oils)
- Cattail seed fluff and down
- Dried shelf fungus (amadou) — produces excellent slow-burning coal
- Fine wood shavings from dry inner wood (never outer bark — it holds moisture)
- Dry pine needles
- Dry leaves, crumbled fine
- Dry animal dung from herbivores
- Man-made: cotton lint, char cloth, shredded cordage, paper

**Kindling:** Larger material that a burning tinder bundle can ignite. Pencil-diameter sticks, dry and dead. Must be genuinely dry — snap-test: dry sticks snap cleanly; damp sticks bend. In wet conditions, seek dry kindling inside deadfall logs (the outer wood absorbs moisture; the inner wood stays dry), under bark, or in dead branches that are still on trees (not yet fallen to the wet ground).

**Fuel:** Thumb-diameter and larger pieces. Once kindling is burning, add fuel progressively larger. Never smother a young fire with fuel too large — you starve it of oxygen and crush it before it can grow.

---

### 4.2 Fire Lays

The arrangement of fuel determines how the fire burns.

**Teepee lay:**
Arrange kindling in a cone shape around your tinder bundle, leaning pieces against each other. Leave a door opening facing the wind (prevailing or available). Add progressively larger fuel sticks in the same cone pattern outside the kindling cone. Light the tinder. The cone channels heat upward and draws air through the base.

*Best for:* Cooking (concentrated heat), getting a fire started quickly, wet conditions (concentrates heat to dry surrounding fuel).

**Log Cabin lay (crisscross):**
Place two large parallel logs as the base. Stack two more logs perpendicular on top. Continue alternating directions like a log cabin. Place tinder and small kindling in the center of the bottom layer.

*Best for:* Sustained heat (longer burn, less tending), large signaling fires, cooking over coals (log cabin collapses into a good coal bed).

**Star lay:**
Place 4-6 large logs radiating outward from a center point like spokes of a wheel. Build the starting fire at the center. Push the logs inward as they burn.

*Best for:* Extended use with minimal fuel management, overnight fires, conserving fuel. The star lay can sustain itself for hours with only occasional attention.

---

### 4.3 Starting Fire With Matches and Lighters

Waterproof matches and windproof lighters are the preferred starting method. Extend their reliability:

- Keep a lighter's fuel reservoir protected from extreme cold (body heat). A cold lighter often fails when a room-temperature lighter works fine.
- Keep matches in a sealed waterproof container (a small pill bottle with a striker glued inside the lid works well).
- A partially used wet match can be dried and used again.
- Even a spent lighter produces sparks from the flint — use them to ignite char cloth or other spark-catching tinder.

---

### 4.4 Friction Fire — Bow Drill Method

The bow drill is the most reliable primitive fire-starting method taught in military survival training. It requires practice before the emergency — do not expect to succeed on the first attempt in a stressful field situation.

**Components:**

*Spindle (drill):* A straight, dry, smooth stick approximately 30 cm long and 2 cm in diameter. Wood should be softer and drier than the fireboard. Good spindle woods: mullein, basswood, willow, cedar, sotol (desert).

*Fireboard:* A flat, dry piece of the same or slightly softer wood than the spindle, approximately 40 cm long, 2-3 cm thick. Cut a circular socket depression in the top surface for the spindle tip. Cut a V-shaped notch from the edge of the board into the socket, extending just past the center of the socket. Place a leaf or piece of bark under the notch to catch the coal.

*Bow:* A curved or slightly bent branch approximately 60-80 cm long, fitted with a cord (paracord, shoelace, or improvised cordage). The cord loops once around the spindle — not too tight, not too loose. Proper tension lets you spin the spindle but does not bind it.

*Handhold (socket):* A piece of wood, bone, rock, or shell with a depression to hold the top of the spindle. This must be harder and smoother than the spindle, or lubricated with sap, animal fat, or dirt, so it does not produce friction at the top (you want friction at the bottom, not both ends).

**Technique:**

1. Kneel on your right knee. Place your left foot on the fireboard, holding it firmly.
2. Place the spindle in the bow cord loop. Put the tip of the spindle in the socket on the fireboard. Hold the handhold against the top of the spindle, pressing firmly down.
3. Brace your left wrist against your left shin (this is critical — it stabilizes the spindle and reduces arm wobble).
4. Using a long, smooth, level stroke, draw the bow back and forth. Maintain consistent downward pressure. Move at a moderate pace — too fast generates speed but no downward pressure; too slow generates pressure but insufficient heat.
5. Continue until you see smoke rising from the notch. This is NOT the coal yet — it is simply heated wood powder.
6. Increase speed and pressure for 15-20 more seconds.
7. Stop. Gently tap the fireboard with a stick to settle the coal in the notch. Carefully lift the fireboard away from the coal.
8. You should see a glowing coal of compressed charred wood powder.
9. Gently fan the coal with one breath. If it glows brighter, it is viable.
10. Slide a leaf or bark under the coal and transfer it to your prepared tinder bundle.
11. Fold the tinder bundle around the coal like a bird's nest wrapping an egg.
12. Hold the bundle up and blow gently and steadily into the base of the coal.
13. The bundle will smoke heavily, then burst into flame.
14. Place the burning bundle into your prepared fire lay immediately.

**Troubleshooting:**
- *No smoke at all:* Wood is wet, spindle-fireboard combination is wrong, or insufficient pressure.
- *Smoke stops when you stop drilling:* The coal is not staying lit — the notch is wrong, wood is damp, or you stopped too soon.
- *The spindle walks out of the socket:* The socket is too shallow or not directly over the notch.
- *Arm fatigue prevents completion:* Two-person method — one operates the bow, the other presses down on the handhold.

---

### 4.5 Flint and Steel / Percussion Sparks

Any hard, crystalline rock struck sharply against steel (or another hard rock) produces sparks. Suitable rocks include flint, chert, quartzite, obsidian, and agate.

The spark must fall onto char cloth or amadou (fungus tinder) to produce a coal. Standard tinder does not catch sparks well — you need material that produces a slow-burning coal when a spark lands on it.

**Making char cloth:** Cut cotton fabric into small squares. Place them in a metal tin with a small hole punched in the lid. Heat over a fire until smoke stops coming from the hole. Remove from heat, let cool completely before opening. The result is partially burned black cotton that catches sparks easily and produces a slow-burning coal.

---

### 4.6 Fire Safety

- Always have a water source or sand nearby to extinguish a fire.
- Clear combustible material 1-2 meters around the fire.
- Never leave a fire unattended.
- To extinguish: drown with water, stir the coals, drown again, test with your hand above (not on) the surface.
- In a survival signaling situation: maintain the fire 24/7. Rescuers may come at any hour.

## Chapter 5: Water Procurement and Purification

Water is your most urgent consumable survival need after immediate threat management. Dehydration impairs judgment before thirst becomes severe — by the time you are very thirsty, you are already mildly impaired.

**Signs of dehydration:**
- Mild (2% body weight loss): Thirst, slightly reduced performance
- Moderate (4-6%): Reduced urine output, dark yellow urine, headache, fatigue, irritability
- Severe (8%+): Confusion, rapid heart rate, inability to stand, medical emergency

**Daily requirement:** In moderate conditions, a minimum of 2 liters per day. In hot conditions or with exertion, 4-6 liters per day.

---

### 5.1 Finding Water

**Terrain indicators:**

*Valleys and low ground:* Water flows downhill. Follow drainages downhill. Low points between hills often contain springs or seeps even when no stream is visible.

*Vegetation:* Lush green vegetation, especially willows, cottonwoods, sycamores, and cattails, indicates a water source. A line of green vegetation across a brown landscape follows a watercourse.

*Animal trails:* Converging animal trails (especially in dry landscapes) often lead to water. Follow deer, cattle, or other herbivore trails downhill.

*Birds:* Many bird species drink and bathe at dawn and dusk. Birds flying low over terrain in a straight line are often going to or from water. Seabirds, ducks, herons, and kingfishers require open water.

*Insects:* Bees and ants require water within a kilometer of their colony. A column of ants moving up a tree may be accessing water trapped in bark.

**Types of water sources:**

*Moving water (streams, rivers):* The most reliable source in most environments. Moving water is generally cleaner than standing water but must still be purified.

*Springs:* Water emerging from the ground is generally clean but should still be purified in case of surface contamination near the source.

*Rainwater:* Collect directly in clean containers or off clean vegetation. Generally does not require purification.

*Dew:* Collect before sunrise by wiping vegetation with absorbent cloth and wringing it into a container. Labor-intensive but reliable in many environments. An hour of dew collection can yield 0.5-1 liter.

*Snow and ice:* Must be melted before drinking. Do not eat snow directly — it requires body heat to melt, accelerating hypothermia. Old blue/gray ice is less salty than white snow ice in arctic environments.

**Water sources to approach with caution:**
- Water downstream from mining activity (heavy metals)
- Water with bright orange/red deposits around it (acid mine drainage)
- Water with unusual color or smell (algae bloom, chemical contamination)
- Water near which dead animals are present

---

### 5.2 Water Collection Methods

**Solar still (ground still):**
Effective in arid regions, time-consuming but requires no fuel.

1. Dig a pit 1 meter wide and 60 cm deep in a low spot or dry wash.
2. Place a container at the bottom center.
3. Optionally, add vegetation around the sides and bottom (not in the container — green plants increase moisture yield).
4. Stretch a plastic sheet across the top of the pit, sealing the edges with soil.
5. Place a small rock in the center of the sheet to create a low point directly above the container.
6. Moisture from the soil and vegetation condenses on the underside of the plastic and drips into the container.
7. Yield: 0.5-1 liter per day in desert conditions.

**Vegetation transpiration bag:**
1. Tie a clear plastic bag over leafy green branches — any non-toxic tree will work.
2. Tie the bag so that the low point (with a rock or small weight) hangs at the corner.
3. Wait several hours. Water transpired by the leaves condenses inside the bag.
4. Drain the pooled water from the low corner.
5. Yield: 0.25-0.5 liters per day per bag.

**Collecting rain:**
Spread any waterproof material — poncho, large leaves arranged on a slope, a bark trough — to direct runoff into a container. Even a light rain on a 1-square-meter surface can yield a liter of water.

---

### 5.3 Water Purification

**All surface water in a survival situation must be purified.** Even clear, clean-looking mountain streams may carry Giardia, Cryptosporidium, or bacterial contamination. Drinking unpurified water risks severe diarrhea, which accelerates dehydration — exactly what you are trying to prevent.

**Method 1: Boiling (most reliable)**

Boiling kills all biological threats: bacteria, viruses, protozoa, and parasites. It does not remove chemical contamination.

- Bring water to a rolling boil (large bubbles continuously rising from the bottom, not just surface simmering).
- Maintain the rolling boil for 1 minute.
- At altitudes above 2,000 meters (6,500 feet), boil for 3 minutes (water boils at a lower temperature at altitude).
- Allow to cool before drinking. Hot water accelerates dehydration.
- Boiled water tastes flat because dissolved gases have been removed. Aerate it by pouring back and forth between containers, or add a pinch of salt.

Making a boiling container without a pot:
- Birch bark folded into a cup shape and pinned with sticks can hold water long enough to boil if the flame does not directly contact the bark above the waterline.
- A green coconut shell, a large clam shell, or a rock depression can hold water for boiling (heat rocks in the fire and drop them into the water — the rock transfers heat directly).
- Line a pit with heavy plastic, pack cloth, or leaves, fill with water, heat stones in the fire and transfer them with improvised tongs (two sticks).

**Method 2: Solar Disinfection (SODIS)**

Effective against bacteria and viruses in clear water. Less effective against Cryptosporidium in turbid water. Requires sunlight.

1. Filter the water to clarity using improvised filtration (see below).
2. Fill clear plastic bottles (PET plastic — labeled with recycling code 1) to the top, eliminating air space.
3. Place in full sun, on a reflective surface if possible (aluminum foil, corrugated metal roof).
4. Exposure time:
   - 6 hours of full sun (UV index 3+)
   - 2 days if overcast
5. Do not use colored or UV-blocking glass or plastic — clear PET only.

This method requires planning and doesn't work at night or in very cloudy conditions, but requires no fuel.

**Method 3: Chemical Treatment**

*Unscented household bleach (5.25-8.25% sodium hypochlorite):*
- Clear water: 8 drops per liter (about 1/8 teaspoon)
- Cloudy water: 16 drops per liter
- Stir, then wait 30 minutes before drinking.
- Water should have a faint chlorine smell after treatment. If not, repeat the dose and wait another 15 minutes.
- Effective against bacteria and viruses. Not effective against Cryptosporidium.

*Iodine tablets:*
- Follow package directions (typically 1-2 tablets per liter)
- Wait 30 minutes (60 minutes in cold water)
- Not recommended for long-term use, pregnant women, or those with thyroid conditions
- Effective against bacteria and viruses

*Potassium permanganate:*
- Can be used as a water purifier and wound antiseptic
- Use very small amounts — water should be light pink, not dark pink
- Dark pink solution is a wound antiseptic, not potable water

**Improvised filtration:**
Filtration removes sediment and improves taste but does NOT purify water by itself. Always follow filtration with boiling or chemical treatment.

Layer in a container with small holes punched in the bottom (or a folded cloth):
1. Grass or cloth (bottom layer — catches large particles)
2. Fine sand
3. Charcoal (from the center of burned logs, not ash — charcoal absorbs some chemicals and improves taste)
4. More fine sand
5. Grass or cloth (top layer — holds the filter layers in place)

Pour water through the top and collect at the bottom. Run water through multiple times for improved clarity.

## Chapter 6: Signaling for Rescue

If rescue is possible, signaling is your highest priority after immediate safety. Most survivors in non-combat emergencies are rescued within 72 hours if they signal effectively. The decision to not signal (to remain hidden or wait) has killed survivors who were within sight of rescuers.

### 6.1 International Distress Signals

**Three is the universal distress signal:** Three of anything — three fires in a triangle, three whistle blasts, three gunshots, three flashes of light — signals distress in international protocol. Rescue personnel are trained to respond to three-repeat signals.

**The international distress signal:**
- 6 blasts per minute (one every 10 seconds) with a whistle
- 6 flashes per minute with a light or mirror
- 6 fires in a triangle (or SOS in Morse)
- Pause 1 minute. Repeat.

**Voice signaling:** Shout only when you hear nearby rescuers. Long-distance shouting exhausts you without benefit. Reserve your voice.

---

### 6.2 Mirror Signaling

A signal mirror is arguably the most effective signaling device available in the field. A standard 5 cm x 7.5 cm mirror can be seen by aircraft at distances exceeding 15 km on a clear day. A piece of a broken CD, an aluminum can lid, or any reflective surface can substitute.

**Technique with a purpose-built signal mirror (with hole):**

1. Hold the mirror a few inches from your face.
2. Extend your other hand at arm's length with two fingers in a V.
3. Look through the sighting hole. Tilt the mirror until the reflected sunlight (bright spot) passes through the sighting hole and falls on your fingers.
4. Move your fingers until the bright spot falls on the target (aircraft or distant observer).
5. Flash by tilting the mirror slightly back and forth — 3 flashes, pause, repeat.

**Technique without a sighting hole:**

1. Hold the mirror at arm's length.
2. Extend a thumb or finger between the mirror and the target.
3. Position the reflected bright spot (visible on your hand) onto your thumb.
4. Move your thumb until it appears to sit on the target — the beam is now roughly aimed.
5. Flash by tilting.

Practice this before you need it. The aim is not intuitive at first. Mirror signaling is most effective when you hear an aircraft before you can see it — begin flashing immediately in the general direction of the sound.

**Always signal toward aircraft even without direct sun.** An overcast sky still produces sufficient diffuse light to create a visible flash on some mirrors.

---

### 6.3 Ground-to-Air Signals

When you have time and materials, construct large visible signals in open ground where they will be visible from aircraft.

**Standard ground-to-air signals (international aviation codes):**

| Symbol | Meaning |
|--------|---------|
| V | Require assistance |
| X | Require medical assistance |
| N | No |
| Y | Yes |
| → | Traveling in this direction |
| SOS | Distress (universal) |

**Construction:** Each symbol should be a minimum of 10 meters long and 3 meters wide to be visible from low-flying aircraft. Use any high-contrast material: dark rocks on snow, bright clothing on dark ground, trampled or cut vegetation arranged in lines, fire ash, or branches.

**Color contrast is critical:** Dark on light (rocks on snow) or light on dark (bright clothing on dark ground). The best signals are those with maximum contrast to the surrounding terrain.

**Maintain signals:** A signal that blows apart or is obscured by snow has no value. Check and refresh your ground signals regularly.

---

### 6.4 Signal Fire

A signal fire must produce maximum visible smoke. Black smoke stands out against green vegetation and overcast sky. White smoke stands out against a dark forest or clear sky.

**For black smoke:** Add rubber, plastic, green leaves, or oil-soaked materials to the fire. These produce dense black smoke.

**For white smoke:** Add green grass, fresh green vegetation, or moss to a hot fire.

**Construction:** The teepee lay (see Chapter 4) produces the best signal fire. Have a pile of green vegetation ready to add when you hear an aircraft. An unlit signal fire that you light on demand is more efficient than a continuous fire that uses all your fuel.

**Three fires in a triangle:** Place signal fires approximately 25 meters apart in a triangle shape. When aircraft are detected, light all three. Three fires is the internationally recognized distress signal.

---

### 6.5 Whistle Code

A whistle is audible to distances of 2-3 km in still conditions and penetrates vegetation far better than the human voice. Carry a whistle in any outdoor situation.

**Distress signal:** 3 blasts, pause, 3 blasts, pause, repeat.

**All clear / I hear you:** 2 blasts.

**Blast duration:** Each blast approximately 3 seconds for maximum audibility.

**Directional signaling:** When you hear a search team's whistle, respond immediately. This helps them triangulate your position. Respond to every call.

## Chapter 7: Navigation Without Instruments

Being able to determine direction, track movement, and navigate toward safety without a compass or GPS is a critical skill. In a survival situation, navigation determines whether you are moving toward safety or deeper into danger.

### 7.1 Sun Shadow Method

**Two-stick method (most accurate):**

This method works anywhere the sun shines and gives true east-west regardless of your latitude.

1. Plant a straight stick (60-90 cm long) vertically in flat ground.
2. Mark the tip of the shadow with a pebble or stick (Point A). This shadow points west in the northern hemisphere in the morning, and east in the afternoon.
3. Wait at least 15 minutes (longer is better).
4. Mark the new shadow tip (Point B).
5. Draw a line connecting A to B.
6. This line runs west (A) to east (B) in the northern hemisphere. It is an east-west line.
7. Stand with your left foot on A and your right foot on B. You are now facing north.

**Why this works:** The sun appears to move from east to west. Shadow tips therefore move from west to east throughout the day. A line connecting two shadow tip positions at different times is always oriented east-west, regardless of the time of day or your latitude (as long as the sun is visible).

**Quick watch method (northern hemisphere):**

1. Hold an analog watch horizontal.
2. Point the hour hand toward the sun.
3. Bisect the angle between the hour hand and the 12 o'clock position.
4. This bisecting line points south (in the northern hemisphere).

*In summer time (daylight saving time):* Use the 1 o'clock position instead of 12.

*Southern hemisphere:* Point the 12 o'clock position at the sun, and bisect the angle between 12 and the hour hand. The bisecting line points north.

---

### 7.2 Night Navigation — Star Methods

**Polaris (North Star) — Northern Hemisphere:**

Polaris sits within 1 degree of true north above the North Pole and barely moves throughout the night. All other stars appear to rotate around it.

Finding Polaris:
1. Find the Big Dipper (Ursa Major) — 7 stars in a dipper shape.
2. Locate the two stars at the outer edge of the dipper bowl (the "pointer stars" — Merak and Dubhe).
3. Follow the line from Merak through Dubhe, extending approximately 5 times the distance between them.
4. The next moderately bright star in that direction is Polaris.
5. Polaris is the last star in the handle of the Little Dipper (Ursa Minor).

**Orion method (works in both hemispheres):**

Orion's Belt (three stars in a straight line) rises due east and sets due west at any latitude. If you watch Orion rise over the horizon, the belt rises almost exactly due east. When the belt is setting, it sets almost exactly due west.

**Southern Cross — Southern Hemisphere:**

The Southern Cross (Crux) — four stars in a cross shape — points toward the southern celestial pole (there is no bright star directly at the south pole).

1. Draw a line through the long axis of the cross (the two stars farthest apart) and extend it approximately 4.5 times the length of the cross.
2. This extended line points toward the south celestial pole.
3. Find the midpoint between the two bright "Pointer" stars (Alpha and Beta Centauri). Draw a line perpendicular to the line connecting them.
4. Where the Southern Cross axis extension and the perpendicular intersect is approximately the south celestial pole.
5. Drop a line from that point to the horizon. The point on the horizon is south.

---

### 7.3 Natural Indicators

Natural indicators are not as accurate as astronomical methods but can help confirm direction when the sky is obscured.

**Vegetation indicators (northern hemisphere):**
- Trees and rocks generally have more moss on their north faces (less sun exposure). Reliable in dense forest; unreliable in open terrain.
- Tree growth rings are often slightly wider on the south side (more sun) — useful if examining a cut stump.
- Sunflowers (genus Helianthus) track the sun and generally face southeast in the morning and southwest in the afternoon.
- Snow on south-facing slopes melts faster than on north-facing slopes. South-facing slopes have less snow, less ice, and earlier spring vegetation.

**Wind patterns:**
In many regions, prevailing winds come from consistent directions. If you know the regional prevailing wind direction (west in much of the continental US, for instance), wind direction can confirm compass bearing. Sand dunes, persistent wave patterns on water, and snow drifts all indicate the prevailing wind direction.

**Terrain reading:**
Rivers generally flow from high to low ground and toward the sea or a large lake. Following a river downstream usually leads to human habitation. Valley ridgelines run roughly parallel to rivers. Stream confluences (where streams join) concentrate population in many regions.

## Chapter 8: Food Procurement

An adult can survive 3 weeks without food. However, food provides energy for maintaining the other survival priorities (gathering firewood, carrying water, building shelter) and is critical for maintaining body temperature in cold environments. In short-term survival (under 72 hours), food is almost never a priority over shelter, water, and signaling.

### 8.1 Universal Edibility Test

When a plant cannot be positively identified as safe, use the Universal Edibility Test. This test takes 24 hours but can prevent fatal plant poisoning.

**Full procedure:**

1. **Separate** the plant into components: roots, stems, leaves, flowers, fruit/berries. Test only one part per test period.

2. **Smell:** Crush a small portion. Strong, unpleasant odors (bitter almonds indicating cyanide, harsh chemical smell) are warning signs. An unremarkable smell does not guarantee safety.

3. **Contact test (skin):** Hold the plant part against the sensitive inner wrist or inside of the elbow for 15 minutes. Redness, burning, or itching indicates a potential irritant — discard.

4. **Lip test:** Place a small piece on your lips. Wait 15 minutes. Burning, stinging, or numbness is a fail — discard.

5. **Tongue test:** Place a small piece on your tongue (do not chew). Wait 15 minutes. Any negative reaction — burning, excessive salivation, bitterness — discard.

6. **Chew test:** Chew a small piece thoroughly. Hold it in your mouth 15 minutes without swallowing. Any negative reaction — discard.

7. **Swallow test:** Swallow the chewed material. Wait 8 hours. Drink water only. If vomiting, diarrhea, or abdominal pain occurs — discard.

8. **Larger portion test:** If no reaction after 8 hours, eat a quarter cup of the same prepared plant. Wait 8 hours again. If no reaction, the plant part is likely safe to eat.

**Limitations:**
- This test identifies many irritants and some toxins, but does not catch all toxic compounds.
- Some highly toxic plants (water hemlock, death camas) produce delayed symptoms.
- Test only one part of one plant at a time. Multiple tests simultaneously prevent identifying the cause of a reaction.
- When in doubt — do not eat.

### 8.2 Basic Hunting Principles

Hunting requires time, energy, and often tools or weapons. The energy expended in a failed hunt may exceed the caloric value of the prey. In survival situations, small animals and passive traps are more efficient than pursuing large game.

**Priority animal targets (most to least effort-efficient):**
1. Fish and shellfish — can be harvested passively (set line fishing)
2. Insects and grubs — high protein, low pursuit effort
3. Small mammals (rabbits, squirrels)
4. Birds
5. Large mammals — not recommended for initial survival due to difficulty

**Fish procurement:**
- Set-line fishing: Attach a baited hook to a line tied to a tree root or stake at water's edge. Set multiple lines. Check them periodically.
- Improvised hooks: Bent wire, thorns, carved wood or bone
- Bait: Worms (found under rocks/logs), insects, grubs, pieces of bright cloth, liver
- Improvised spear fishing: Sharpen a long green sapling to a point and harden the tip briefly in fire embers. Wade slowly in clear, still water. Aim below the fish — light refraction makes fish appear shallower than they are.
- Improvised net: Weave green branches or vines to form a funnel-shaped weir across a stream. Fish swimming downstream become trapped.

**Insects:**
High in protein and fat, insects are abundant and easily gathered. Avoid brightly colored insects (warning coloration), hairy insects (potential skin irritants), and disease vectors (mosquitoes, ticks, flies). Safe options include grasshoppers, crickets, ants, termites, and grubs. Remove wings and legs before eating. Cook if possible — many parasites are killed by cooking.

---

### 8.3 Trap Types

Traps work while you sleep or perform other tasks. A well-placed trap can provide food with minimal energy expenditure. Set at least 10-12 traps to improve your odds — a single trap rarely produces.

**Placement:** Near obvious animal sign: game trails (look for pressed grass, worn dirt), latrines (droppings), burrow entrances, gnawed vegetation (rabbits, beavers), food sources (berry patches, grain fields, nut trees).

**Figure-4 Deadfall Trap:**

A deadfall trap uses a heavy rock or log balanced over bait, supported by three interlocking sticks. When the animal disturbs the bait, the sticks collapse and the weight falls on the animal.

Components:
- *Upright post* (vertical stick): Cut a notch on one side near the top, and a diagonal point at the bottom (to stick into the ground). The notch holds the diagonal stick.
- *Diagonal stick* (angled at ~45 degrees): Notched at the top to engage the upright notch, and shaped at the lower end to hook the toggle stick. Bait is placed or tied to the diagonal stick.
- *Toggle (horizontal) stick*: Sits under the rock/weight. One end engages the lower end of the diagonal stick; the other end holds the weight.

Assembly: The system is under tension — the weight of the deadfall is balanced by the interlocking notches. When an animal disturbs the bait on the diagonal stick, the toggle disengages and the weight falls.

Building notes:
- The rock must be 2-3 times heavier than the target animal.
- All notch cuts must be clean and smooth for reliable release.
- Practice the figure-4 assembly before you need it — the geometry is not intuitive.

**Spring Snare:**

Uses a bent sapling's stored energy to jerk a noose upward when triggered, catching a small animal around the neck.

Components:
- A springy sapling or branch (the spring)
- Cordage for the snare (wire is ideal; improvised from plant fibers, inner bark, paracord)
- A trigger mechanism (a notched stick combination)
- A noose sized for the target animal

Placement: On an active game trail. The noose should be suspended at head height for the target animal (fist height for rabbits; ankle height for smaller rodents). The noose diameter should be slightly larger than the animal's head.

Operation: The snare cord runs from the noose, along the trail, through the trigger mechanism, to the bent-down spring. When an animal steps through the noose, it triggers the release, the spring jerks upward, and the noose tightens around the animal's neck or body.

**Ethical note on traps:** Check traps every 4-6 hours. A trapped animal may escape if left too long, or may be taken by another predator. A trapped animal left alive suffers unnecessarily.

## Chapter 9: First Aid in Survival Situations

In a survival situation, professional medical care may be unavailable for days. The goal of survival first aid is to keep the casualty alive and functional until evacuation or rescue. FM 21-76 emphasizes stabilization over sophisticated treatment.

### 9.1 Wound Treatment

**Control bleeding first — it is the most immediately life-threatening injury.**

**Direct pressure:**
Apply firm, continuous pressure with the cleanest available material directly over the wound. Do not lift the dressing to check — this disrupts clot formation. If blood soaks through, add more material on top and maintain pressure. Hold for a minimum of 10-15 minutes.

**Elevation:**
Raise the injured limb above heart level while maintaining direct pressure. This reduces blood pressure at the wound.

**Pressure points:**
For bleeding from an arm: press the brachial artery against the humerus on the inner upper arm.
For bleeding from a leg: press the femoral artery against the femur in the groin crease.

**Tourniquet (for limb wounds that will not stop bleeding with direct pressure):**
A tourniquet stops life-threatening hemorrhage from a limb by cutting off blood supply to everything below it. It will cause tissue death if left on too long, but exsanguination (bleeding to death) kills faster.

Improvised tourniquet:
1. Use material 5 cm (2 inches) wide — narrower material (like rope) causes tissue damage without effectively stopping blood flow.
2. Apply 5-8 cm above the wound (not over a joint).
3. Wrap twice around the limb, tie a half-hitch over a short stick or pen.
4. Twist the stick until bleeding stops.
5. Tie the stick in place with the remaining ends of the material.
6. Write the time of application on the skin with a marker or blood — or use the patient's forehead.
7. Never cover a tourniquet. It must remain visible.
8. Once applied, do not remove in the field — removal causes a surge of toxins from dying tissue and can cause cardiac arrest. Removal is a hospital procedure.

**Wound cleaning:**
Irrigation with clean water under pressure (squirt from a container with a small hole, or use a syringe) is the most effective field decontamination. Flush copiously — the goal is mechanical removal of bacteria and debris. Do not use hydrogen peroxide in the wound — it damages healthy tissue along with the bacteria. A very dilute iodine solution (pale tea color) can be used for wound irrigation.

**Wound closure:**
In a survival situation, close wounds only if:
- The wound edges can be brought together without tension
- The wound is clearly clean (no debris remaining after irrigation)
- It is a facial wound (face wounds have excellent blood supply and resist infection)
- More than 4-6 hours will pass before medical care is available AND the wound is recent AND clean

Leave contaminated wounds open. Closing a contaminated wound seals bacteria in, causing infection.

Closure methods:
- Steri-strips (preferred if available)
- Butterfly bandage (improvised from tape)
- Sutures (only if trained)
- Leave open with loose packing for drain: place sterile gauze loosely in the wound to allow drainage and change it daily

### 9.2 Splinting

The goal of a field splint is immobilization of a fracture to prevent movement that causes pain, increases injury, and makes the victim non-ambulatory.

**General principles:**
- Immobilize the joint above and below the fracture.
- Pad the splint (never apply rigid material directly to skin — friction sores develop quickly).
- Check circulation, sensation, and movement (CSM) below the splint before and after application.
- If the limb is pulseless, cold, or blue below the fracture, the bone may be compressing a blood vessel — splint anyway but transport as emergency.

**Improvised splint construction:**
- Rigid component: straight sticks, branches, folded cardboard, rolled magazines
- Padding: clothing, leaves, moss
- Binding: strips of cloth, paracord, torn clothing

**Lower leg/ankle fracture:**
Fold a bulky jacket or sleeping bag into a U-shape and wrap around the leg. Bind gently above and below the fracture.

**Arm fracture:**
Splint the arm against the body, or use two padded rigid pieces on either side. Sling the arm across the chest and tie to the body to prevent movement.

**Femur (thigh) fracture:**
This is a life-threatening injury — the thigh can hold 2-4 liters of blood in internal hemorrhage, and a femur fracture victim can bleed to death without an obvious wound. Improvise a traction splint: tie padded poles to the body and leg, and pull gentle traction on the foot (which reduces the internal bleeding and spasm) before binding. Transport is urgent.

### 9.3 Heat Injuries

**Heat cramps:**
Painful muscle cramps from salt loss during sweating. Treatment: move to cool environment, rest, drink water with electrolytes (a pinch of salt dissolved in water if nothing else is available).

**Heat exhaustion:**
Heavy sweating, pale/moist/cool skin, weakness, dizziness, headache, nausea, elevated heart rate. The victim is still able to sweat.

Treatment:
- Move to cool shade
- Loosen or remove clothing
- Cool with water misting or wet cloths
- Have the patient lie down with legs elevated
- Provide cool water to drink (small amounts frequently if nauseous)
- Rest for at least 24-48 hours before returning to activity

**Heat stroke — medical emergency:**
Hot skin (may be dry or sweaty), altered mental status (confusion, slurred speech, combativeness, seizures, loss of consciousness), elevated body temperature over 40°C (104°F).

Treatment:
- Aggressive cooling is the treatment — begin immediately
- Move to shade
- Remove all clothing
- Immerse in cool water if available, or wet all skin surfaces and fan vigorously
- Pack ice or cold water against neck, armpits, and groin (major blood vessels)
- Continue cooling until mental status improves
- Transport immediately — heat stroke is fatal without medical care

---

### 9.4 Cold Injuries

**Hypothermia:**
Core body temperature drops below 35°C (95°F). The body's heating mechanisms begin to fail.

Stages:
- *Mild (35-32°C / 95-89°F):* Shivering, confusion, clumsiness, pale skin, rapid heart rate. Victim can still rewarm with proper treatment.
- *Moderate (32-28°C / 89-82°F):* Shivering stops (dangerous sign — the body can no longer generate heat). Increasing confusion, muscle rigidity.
- *Severe (<28°C / <82°F):* Unconsciousness, very slow or absent pulse, very slow or absent breathing. May appear dead.

Treatment (mild to moderate):
- Remove from cold and wind
- Remove wet clothing
- Insulate from ground
- Cover entirely (including head)
- Add heat to the groin, armpits, and neck (warm water bottles, body contact)
- Provide warm sweet liquids if conscious (never alcohol — it dilates blood vessels and increases heat loss)
- Do not rub the extremities — this drives cold blood to the core and can cause cardiac arrest

Treatment (severe):
- Handle very gently — cardiac irritability is extreme; rough handling can trigger ventricular fibrillation
- Rewarm slowly with passive heat (insulation, warm environment)
- Even a victim who appears dead may survive with aggressive hospital rewarming — "Not dead until warm and dead"

**Frostbite:**
Ice crystal formation in tissues. Affects extremities (fingers, toes, ears, nose, cheeks) first.

Stages:
- *Frostnip:* Skin is red, slightly numb, then white. No blisters. Rewarm by skin-to-skin contact. Complete recovery expected.
- *Superficial frostbite:* Skin is white/grayish, firm on surface but soft below. May blister. Rewarm with warm water (38-42°C / 100-107°F) — never hotter. Do not rub. Protect blisters.
- *Deep frostbite:* Tissue is hard, cold, insensate. May be black. Extremely serious.

Critical rule for frostbite: **Do not rewarm if there is any chance of refreezing.** It is better to walk on frozen feet than to rewarm them, walk on them again, and refreeze. Refreezing after thawing causes catastrophically greater damage than leaving them frozen.

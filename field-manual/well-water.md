# Private Well Water Guide

**Source:** EPA Private Water Systems guidance and EPA publications. US Government work, public domain per 17 U.S.C. § 105.

---

## Introduction

Approximately 13% of Americans — more than 43 million people — rely on private wells for their household water supply. Unlike municipal water systems, private wells are not subject to US EPA oversight under the Safe Drinking Water Act. Well owners are responsible for testing, maintaining, and treating their own water supply.

This guide is based on EPA guidance documents for private well owners, produced by the US Environmental Protection Agency (a US Government agency). All EPA publications are US Government works in the public domain per 17 U.S.C. § 105.

**EPA Safe Drinking Water Hotline:** 800-426-4791
Available Monday-Friday, 10 AM-4 PM Eastern. Information on finding certified labs, contaminants, and treatment options.

## Chapter 1: Why Test Your Well Water

Municipal water systems are required by law to test water regularly and report results. Private well owners have no such mandate. Contamination of a private well can go undetected for years, causing chronic health effects that are often not traced to the water source.

### 1.1 The Fundamental Problem

Well water quality is determined by:
- **Geology:** What rock and soil the water passes through on its way to the aquifer
- **Depth and construction of the well:** Shallower wells are more vulnerable to surface contamination
- **Surrounding land use:** Agricultural, industrial, and residential activities affect groundwater
- **Well integrity:** Cracks in casing, faulty seals, and aged infrastructure allow contamination to enter

Water can look perfectly clear, smell fine, and taste normal while containing contaminants that cause serious health effects over months or years. The most dangerous well contaminants — arsenic, radon, nitrates — are colorless, odorless, and tasteless.

### 1.2 Major Contamination Sources

**Biological contamination:**
- Septic systems (especially when sited too close to the well or malfunctioning)
- Agricultural waste (animal feeding operations, manure application)
- Flood events (carry surface bacteria and pathogens into the well)
- Insects, small animals, and surface water entering through damaged well caps

**Agricultural contamination:**
- Fertilizers (nitrates, phosphates)
- Pesticides and herbicides
- Irrigation return water

**Industrial and mining contamination:**
- Heavy metals from mining (arsenic, lead, mercury, chromium)
- Volatile organic compounds (solvents, petroleum products from leaking underground storage tanks)
- Industrial chemicals

**Geological contamination (natural):**
- Arsenic (naturally occurring in many rock formations, especially in the Southwest, New England, and Midwest)
- Radon (radioactive gas from uranium decay, especially in granite-heavy geology)
- Fluoride (beneficial in small amounts; toxic at high concentrations)
- Iron, manganese, hardness minerals (aesthetic issues, not typically health threats at low levels)
- Naturally occurring nitrates

## Chapter 2: Common Contaminants and Health Effects

### 2.1 Coliform Bacteria

**What it is:** Coliform bacteria are a broad group of bacteria found in the environment and in the intestinal tracts of humans and animals. *Total coliform* includes environmental coliforms not specifically associated with fecal contamination. *Fecal coliform* (including *E. coli*) specifically indicates recent fecal contamination.

**Health effects:** Coliform bacteria themselves may not cause illness, but their presence indicates a pathway for contamination. *E. coli* O157:H7 and other pathogenic strains cause severe gastrointestinal illness (bloody diarrhea, vomiting, kidney failure in severe cases). Infants, elderly, and immunocompromised individuals are at highest risk.

**EPA Maximum Contaminant Level (MCL):** Zero total coliform per 100 mL. Any detection of coliform in a well is a concern requiring investigation.

**Sources:** Faulty well seal, surface water infiltration, nearby septic system, flooding.

**Treatment:** Shock chlorination (see Chapter 5). If contamination recurs, investigate the source (well construction, septic proximity).

---

### 2.2 Nitrates

**What it is:** Nitrate (NO₃⁻) is a form of nitrogen common in fertilizers and animal waste. It is highly soluble in water and moves freely through soil into groundwater.

**Health effects:** Nitrate itself is not directly toxic at moderate levels, but in the human gut (and especially in infant guts), it is converted to nitrite, which reacts with hemoglobin to form methemoglobin — a form that cannot carry oxygen. This causes **methemoglobinemia ("blue baby syndrome")** in infants under 6 months, which can be fatal.

In adults, high nitrate is associated with increased risk of some cancers with long-term exposure, but the most acute risk is in infants.

**EPA MCL:** 10 mg/L as nitrogen (10 ppm). Do not use water above 10 ppm nitrate for formula preparation for infants under 6 months.

**Sources:** Agricultural fertilizer runoff, septic systems, animal feedlots.

**Treatment:** Reverse osmosis or ion exchange. Boiling does NOT remove nitrates — it concentrates them. Do not use high-nitrate water for infant formula under any circumstances.

---

### 2.3 Lead

**What it is:** Lead is a heavy metal that enters drinking water almost exclusively from plumbing, not from the well or aquifer itself. Lead solder used in plumbing joints (prior to 1986), lead pipes in older homes, and brass fixtures can leach lead into standing water.

**Health effects:** Lead is a neurotoxin with no safe level for children. Effects include: developmental delays, learning disabilities, behavioral problems, hearing loss, and growth problems. Adults with chronic high exposure experience hypertension, kidney damage, and fertility issues.

**EPA action level:** 15 ppb (micrograms per liter). At or above this level in tap water, action is required.

**Sources:** Old plumbing (pre-1986 construction especially), lead-soldered joints, leaded brass fixtures.

**Treatment:** Run the cold water tap for 2 minutes before drawing water for drinking or cooking if water has been standing in pipes (especially after overnight or extended non-use). This flushes the standing water from pipes. Install a point-of-use filter certified for lead removal (NSF/ANSI Standard 53). Replace lead-containing plumbing.

**Testing note:** Because lead comes from plumbing, not the aquifer, a sample drawn from a garden hose tap (bypassing indoor plumbing) may show very different results than a first-draw sample from the kitchen tap.

---

### 2.4 Arsenic

**What it is:** Arsenic is a metalloid that occurs naturally in many rock formations, particularly in the western US, New England, and parts of the Midwest. It can also be introduced by industrial activity and some pesticides.

**Health effects:** Long-term exposure to arsenic causes:
- Increased risk of bladder, lung, kidney, and skin cancers
- Cardiovascular disease
- Peripheral neuropathy (numbness/tingling in extremities)
- Skin changes (hyperkeratosis)

Arsenic is a confirmed Group 1 carcinogen (definitely causes cancer in humans).

**EPA MCL:** 10 ppb (µg/L).

**Sources:** Natural geological deposits (primary source for most wells), historical pesticide use (orchards, vineyards), industrial sites, mining.

**Treatment:** Reverse osmosis, activated alumina filtration, or ion exchange. Point-of-use reverse osmosis systems are effective and affordable for the kitchen tap.

**Geographic risk:** Wells in the western US (particularly the Basin and Range region), New England (granite-heavy geology), parts of the upper Midwest, and along the Atlantic Coastal Plain should be tested for arsenic. Check EPA's private wells map (epa.gov/privatewells) for your region's specific risks.

---

### 2.5 Radon

**What it is:** Radon (Rn-222) is a naturally occurring radioactive gas formed by the decay of uranium in soil and rock. It is colorless and odorless.

**Health effects:** Radon in air is the second leading cause of lung cancer in the United States (after cigarette smoking), accounting for approximately 21,000 deaths per year according to EPA estimates. Radon in water contributes to indoor air radon levels (radon volatilizes from water during showers, dishwashing, etc.).

**EPA recommendation:** Test water if indoor air radon levels are elevated. EPA's threshold for water radon is 300 pCi/L, though no safe level has been established.

**Sources:** Natural geological uranium decay, especially in granite-heavy formations (New England, Appalachia, upper Midwest).

**Treatment:** Aeration systems or granular activated carbon (GAC) filters at the point of entry (whole-house).

**Testing note:** Radon in water testing requires a specialized test — standard water tests do not include radon. Request it specifically.

---

### 2.6 Fluoride

**What it is:** Fluoride (F⁻) occurs naturally in groundwater at varying concentrations depending on local geology.

**Health effects:**
- Beneficial at low concentrations (0.7 mg/L — prevents dental cavities)
- Dental fluorosis (mottling of tooth enamel) at 1.5-4 mg/L with long-term exposure in children
- Skeletal fluorosis (bone pain, joint problems) at sustained exposures above 4 mg/L

**EPA MCL:** 4 mg/L (MCL for health); 2 mg/L secondary standard (aesthetic/cosmetic concern for tooth enamel in children).

**Treatment:** Reverse osmosis, activated alumina, or bone char filters reduce fluoride.

---

### 2.7 Volatile Organic Compounds (VOCs)

**What it is:** VOCs include a wide range of chemicals including solvents (trichloroethylene, tetrachloroethylene), MTBE (a gasoline additive), benzene, toluene, and others.

**Sources:** Leaking underground storage tanks (gas stations), industrial sites, landfills, dry cleaning facilities.

**Health effects:** Vary by compound; many are carcinogenic. Benzene is a known leukemia-causing agent.

**Treatment:** Activated carbon filtration (point-of-entry for whole-house or point-of-use for drinking water only).

**Testing:** If your well is within 1 mile of a gas station, dry cleaner, industrial facility, or known leaking storage tank site, test specifically for VOCs.

## Chapter 3: Visual, Smell, and Taste Indicators

Well water problems sometimes announce themselves through observable characteristics. These clues are useful for prioritizing testing but do not replace laboratory analysis — many dangerous contaminants have no sensory warning.

| Observation | Possible cause(s) | Significance |
|-------------|------------------|--------------|
| Turbidity (cloudiness) | Sediment, bacterial bloom, stirred sediment | Tests immediately required; biological or physical contamination |
| Yellow/brown color | Iron, manganese, tannins (organic matter) | Not typically a health concern at low levels; aesthetic problem |
| Green/blue color | Copper corrosion from pipes | Copper levels may be elevated; plumbing issue |
| Reddish/orange staining on fixtures | Iron (common), manganese | Iron bacteria possible; aesthetic issue; test for iron |
| Black staining | Manganese | Elevated manganese; test recommended |
| "Rotten egg" (sulfur/H₂S) smell | Hydrogen sulfide (from sulfur-reducing bacteria OR from natural geological sources) | Not directly a health hazard from H₂S itself, but may indicate other contamination; biological if bacterial |
| "Bleach" smell | Chlorine (if your water is on a system) or chloramine treatment | Generally indicates treated water, normal |
| Musty/earthy smell | Iron or sulfur bacteria | Aesthetic; test for biological contamination |
| Gasoline or petroleum smell | VOC contamination (fuel leak nearby) | Serious. Do not use water; test immediately |
| Oily sheen | Petroleum products, iron bacteria | Test for VOCs and biological contamination |
| Slime in toilet tank | Iron or sulfur bacteria | Not a direct health risk; bacteria reduce iron/sulfur |
| No taste, color, smell | Nitrates, arsenic, radon, lead, many VOCs | NORMAL DOES NOT MEAN SAFE for these contaminants |

## Chapter 4: When to Test

### 4.1 Annual Testing

**Test annually for:**
- Total coliform bacteria and E. coli
- Nitrates

These two tests form the baseline for any private well. They are relatively inexpensive ($25-75 combined at most state-certified labs) and cover the most common health threats.

### 4.2 Initial Testing for a New Well or Home

When first occupying a home with a private well, test for:
- Total coliform/E. coli
- Nitrates
- pH (affects corrosion)
- Total dissolved solids (TDS, a general water quality indicator)
- Hardness
- Iron and manganese
- Arsenic (if in a risk region)
- Lead (especially if pre-1986 construction)
- Radon (if in a granite/uranium-bearing geology region)
- VOCs (if near industrial or fueling sites)

A comprehensive initial test panel costs $150-400 depending on the lab and parameters included.

### 4.3 Test After These Events

- **After a flood:** Bacteria and nitrates commonly enter wells during floods. Test within 2 weeks of floodwater receding.
- **After any nearby land use change:** New agricultural activity, industrial development, septic system installation.
- **After repairs to the well or plumbing.**
- **If neighbors report contamination.**
- **When you notice a new smell, taste, or color in the water.**
- **If anyone in the household has unexplained gastrointestinal illness.**
- **After any period of non-use (vacation home):** Stagnant water in pipes and wells can accumulate bacteria.

## Chapter 5: Shock Chlorination — EPA Standard Method

Shock chlorination is the EPA-recommended method for disinfecting a private well that has tested positive for coliform bacteria, or as a precautionary measure after flooding, flooding, or well repair. It uses ordinary household bleach (sodium hypochlorite) to deliver a high concentration of chlorine throughout the well and distribution system to kill bacteria.

### 5.1 Before You Begin

**Identify:**
- Well depth (in feet)
- Well diameter (in inches) — typically 4, 6, or 8 inches for drilled wells; wider for dug wells

**You will need:**
- Unscented liquid chlorine bleach (5.25-8.25% sodium hypochlorite) — Clorox Regular or equivalent. Do NOT use scented bleach, bleach with surfactants, or color-safe bleach.
- A clean bucket and hose (garden hose attached to an outdoor tap works)
- Protective gloves and eye protection
- A means to calculate how much bleach to add (see table below)

---

### 5.2 Calculating Bleach Volume

The goal is to achieve approximately 200 ppm (200 mg/L) chlorine concentration in the well water. The volume of water in a well is determined by the depth of water in the well (not the total well depth) and the well diameter.

**Volume of water in a well (gallons):**

The formula:
```
Gallons = (Diameter in inches)² × 0.0408 × (Water depth in feet)
```

Examples:
- 6-inch diameter, 50 feet of water: 6² × 0.0408 × 50 = 36 × 0.0408 × 50 = 73 gallons
- 6-inch diameter, 100 feet of water: 6² × 0.0408 × 100 = 147 gallons
- 8-inch diameter, 100 feet of water: 8² × 0.0408 × 100 = 261 gallons

**Bleach required (using 6% sodium hypochlorite bleach):**

| Gallons of water in well | Bleach needed (6% NaOCl) |
|--------------------------|--------------------------|
| 50 gallons | 1 pint (2 cups) |
| 100 gallons | 1 quart (4 cups) |
| 200 gallons | ½ gallon (8 cups) |
| 300 gallons | ¾ gallon (12 cups) |
| 400 gallons | 1 gallon (16 cups) |
| 500 gallons | 1.25 gallons |

For 8-10% bleach (concentrated), use about 75% of the above quantities.

---

### 5.3 Shock Chlorination Procedure

**Step 1: Prepare the chlorine solution.**
Dissolve the calculated amount of bleach in 5-10 gallons of water in a clean bucket. (Diluting before adding helps distribute the chlorine better throughout the well.)

**Step 2: Remove the well cap.**
Use appropriate tools. Inspect the cap and casing for visible damage.

**Step 3: Pour the chlorine solution into the well.**
Pour slowly. If you can reach the water surface, use a hose to mix the solution into the water column.

**Step 4: Circulate the chlorinated water.**
Connect a hose from an outdoor tap to direct water back into the well (recirculate). Run this for 15-30 minutes to ensure thorough distribution of chlorine throughout the water column.

**Step 5: Open all taps in the house.**
Working from the most distant taps, open cold water taps throughout the house. Let water run until you can smell chlorine at each tap — this indicates the chlorinated well water has reached every fixture. Then close each tap.

**Step 6: Let the chlorine sit.**
Allow the chlorinated water to sit undisturbed for a minimum of 8 hours. Overnight (12-24 hours) is preferred. Do not use any water during this period.

**Step 7: Flush the system.**
After the contact time, flush the well and plumbing system completely. Run water from an outdoor tap (do not route heavily chlorinated water to your septic system — it can disrupt the bacterial process) until you can no longer detect chlorine smell. This typically takes several hours to an entire day depending on well volume.

**Step 8: Do NOT use water for drinking until retested.**
Wait at least 1 week (to allow any remaining traces of chlorine to dissipate), then collect a water sample and send to a certified lab. Shock chlorination is successful when a post-treatment sample tests negative for coliform bacteria.

**If bacteria persist after shock chlorination:**
- Repeat the shock chlorination with a higher concentration
- Investigate the contamination source: cracked casing, improperly sealed well cap, surface water drainage pattern, nearby septic system
- Consult a licensed well driller or water treatment professional

## Chapter 6: Emergency Water Treatment

In an emergency situation where well water cannot be tested, or where normal water supplies are unavailable, the following methods reduce biological contamination risk. These methods do not remove chemical contaminants.

### 6.1 Boiling

**Most reliable method for biological contamination.**

- Bring water to a rolling boil (vigorous bubbles breaking the surface from the bottom).
- Maintain the rolling boil for **1 minute** at elevations below 6,500 feet (2,000 meters).
- At elevations above 6,500 feet: boil for **3 minutes** (water boils at lower temperatures at altitude, requiring longer time to achieve equivalent pasteurization).
- Allow to cool in a covered container.
- Store in a clean, covered container.

**Boiling kills:** All bacteria, viruses, protozoa (Giardia, Cryptosporidium), and parasites.

**Boiling does NOT remove:** Chemical contaminants (nitrates, arsenic, lead, VOCs), heavy metals, salt. Boiling actually concentrates these contaminants as water evaporates.

---

### 6.2 Chlorine Bleach (Emergency Treatment)

Use **unscented liquid chlorine bleach** (5.25-8.25% sodium hypochlorite).

**Treatment doses:**

| Water volume | 6% bleach (clear water) | 6% bleach (cloudy water) |
|-------------|------------------------|--------------------------|
| 1 quart | 2 drops | 4 drops |
| 1 liter | 2 drops | 4 drops |
| 1 gallon | 8 drops (approx. 1/8 tsp) | 16 drops |
| 5 gallons | 40 drops (approx. ½ tsp) | 80 drops |

**Procedure:**
1. If the water is cloudy (turbid), filter through clean cloth first and allow sediment to settle.
2. Add the calculated drops of bleach.
3. Stir thoroughly.
4. Wait **30 minutes** before drinking.
5. The treated water should have a slight chlorine smell. If it does not, repeat the dose and wait another 15 minutes.
6. If there is still no chlorine smell after the second dose, the water may be too heavily contaminated for bleach to be effective. Boiling is required.

**Effectiveness:** Bleach kills bacteria and viruses. It is NOT reliably effective against Cryptosporidium (requires boiling or filtration to 1 micron or smaller).

---

### 6.3 Iodine Tablets

Iodine tablets (tetraglycine hydroperiodide) are commonly available at outdoor and sporting goods stores.

**Standard dose:** 1 tablet per liter of clear water (2 tablets for cloudy water).

**Wait time:** 30 minutes (60 minutes if water is cold or cloudy).

**Effectiveness:** Kills bacteria and viruses. Less effective against Cryptosporidium.

**Limitations:**
- Not recommended for pregnant women (may affect fetal thyroid)
- Not recommended for people with thyroid conditions
- Not recommended for long-term use (more than a few weeks)
- Leaves an iodine taste (Vitamin C or sodium thiosulfate can neutralize taste after the contact time)

---

### 6.4 Commercial Water Filters

Portable water filters (Sawyer Squeeze, Katadyn, LifeStraw, etc.) use a hollow-fiber or ceramic membrane to physically remove bacteria and protozoa by filtration.

**Key specification:** Filter pore size of 0.1-0.2 microns is required to remove Giardia and Cryptosporidium.

**Effectiveness by pore size:**
- 0.1 micron: Removes bacteria, Giardia, Cryptosporidium. Does NOT remove viruses.
- Add chemical treatment (iodine or chlorine) after filtration for complete disinfection when virus risk is high (sewage contamination, international travel).

**Activated carbon filters** (Brita, PUR, ZeroWater) improve taste and remove some chemicals but do NOT reliably remove bacteria, viruses, or protozoa. Do not use a carbon filter as a disinfection method.

**Reverse osmosis filters** (whole-house or countertop): Remove bacteria, viruses, protozoa, and many chemicals including arsenic, nitrates, fluoride, and most heavy metals. Slow flow rate; waste water. Not portable.

## Chapter 7: Well Maintenance and Annual Inspection

### 7.1 Annual Inspection Checklist

Perform this inspection every spring and after any flood or significant weather event.

**Well cap and seal:**
- [ ] Well cap is present and undamaged
- [ ] Cap sits flush without gaps; no cracks visible
- [ ] Cap is properly secured (insects and surface water should not be able to enter)
- [ ] Ventilation hole in cap is screened and clear (if present)

**Well casing:**
- [ ] Casing extends at least 30 cm (12 inches) above the ground surface
- [ ] Casing has no visible cracks, holes, or deterioration
- [ ] Casing is free of efflorescence (white mineral deposits that indicate water infiltration from outside)

**Surrounding area:**
- [ ] Ground slopes away from the casing — no pooling of surface water near the well
- [ ] No standing water or flooding evidence within 3 meters of the well
- [ ] No chemical storage (pesticides, fuel, fertilizer) within 15 meters of the well
- [ ] No livestock corrals or heavy animal concentration within 30 meters of the well
- [ ] No septic system or leach field within minimum setback distance (check local codes — typically 15-30 meters minimum from septic tank, 15 meters from septic field)

**Electrical (if submersible pump):**
- [ ] Pump runs smoothly; no unusual sounds or vibration
- [ ] Water pressure is consistent
- [ ] No evidence of water intrusion around the pump wiring connections

**Pressure tank (if present):**
- [ ] Pressure cycles between normal limits (typically 30-50 psi or 40-60 psi depending on setting)
- [ ] Pump does not short-cycle (turn on and off more than twice per minute — indicates waterlogged pressure tank)

---

### 7.2 Minimum Setback Distances (EPA General Guidance)

These are general EPA guidance values. Your state or county may have stricter requirements.

| Potential contamination source | Minimum distance from well |
|-------------------------------|---------------------------|
| Septic tank | 15 meters (50 feet) |
| Septic leach field | 15-30 meters (50-100 feet) |
| Cesspool / pit privy | 30 meters (100 feet) |
| Animal feedlot | 30-60 meters (100-200 feet) |
| Fuel storage tank (underground) | 30 meters (100 feet) |
| Road salt storage | 30 meters (100 feet) |
| Agricultural chemical mixing area | 30 meters (100 feet) |

If your well is within these setback distances, increase testing frequency and scope.

---

### 7.3 Finding a Certified Water Testing Laboratory

All water testing for health-related contaminants should be performed by a state-certified laboratory. Certified labs follow quality assurance procedures that home testing kits cannot match.

**How to find a certified lab:**

1. **EPA Safe Drinking Water Hotline:** 800-426-4791. Can direct you to your state's certification program and list of certified labs.

2. **State drinking water program:** Each state has its own program. Search "[your state] certified drinking water laboratory" or contact your state health department.

3. **EPA online directory:** epa.gov/privatewells — links to state programs.

**What to ask the lab:**
- What containers do I use for each test?
- How should I collect the sample?
- How long do I have between collection and delivery?
- Do I bring it to you, or do you provide pickup?

**Sample collection:** Follow the lab's instructions exactly. Improper sample collection (using a non-sterile container, touching the inside of the sample bottle, running the tap for the wrong amount of time, or failing to refrigerate the sample) can produce false results — both false positives (showing contamination that isn't there) and false negatives (missing contamination that is there).

## Chapter 8: Special Situations

### 8.1 After a Flood

Floodwaters carry bacteria, chemical spills, and debris into wells that are inundated or near-inundated. Do not assume your well is safe after flooding even if it appears undamaged.

**Immediate steps:**
1. Do not use the well until testing is complete.
2. Inspect the well casing for physical damage.
3. If the well was submerged, pump it down (run water until it runs clear and smells/looks normal).
4. Contact your local health department — they may offer free or subsidized post-flood testing.
5. Perform shock chlorination (Chapter 5).
6. Test at least 1 week after chlorination is complete (to allow residual chlorine to dissipate).

**What to test for after flooding:** Total coliform, E. coli, nitrates (agricultural floodwaters), and any chemicals associated with nearby land use.

---

### 8.2 Dug Wells vs. Drilled Wells

**Dug wells** (hand-dug, large-diameter, typically less than 10 meters deep):
- Much more vulnerable to surface contamination than drilled wells
- Require annual bacterial testing at minimum
- Shock chlorinate annually as a precaution if near potential contamination sources
- Should be upgraded to a drilled well if contamination is recurring

**Drilled wells** (machine-drilled, narrow diameter, typically 30-150 meters deep):
- More protected by the well casing and depth
- Still vulnerable to contamination through casing defects, at the surface, or from naturally occurring geological contaminants
- Test annually for bacteria/nitrates; less frequent testing for stable geological contaminants (arsenic, radon) unless test results indicate a problem

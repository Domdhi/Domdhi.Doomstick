# Amateur Radio — FCC Part 97 Reference Guide

**Source:** FCC Title 47 CFR Part 97 (Amateur Radio Service regulations). US Government work, public domain per 17 U.S.C. § 105.

---

## Introduction

Title 47 of the Code of Federal Regulations (CFR), Part 97, governs Amateur Radio Service in the United States. Federal regulations produced by US Government agencies are not subject to copyright under 17 U.S.C. § 105 and are in the public domain.

This reference document covers the key provisions of Part 97, band plans, operating procedures, emergency communication protocols, and practical reference tables for amateur radio operators.

---

## Chapter 1: Purpose of the Amateur Radio Service

### §97.1 — Basis and Purpose

The amateur and amateur-satellite services are for qualified persons interested in radio technique solely with a personal aim and without pecuniary interest. These purposes and the rules and regulations in this part are intended to provide an amateur radio service having a fundamental purpose as expressed in the following principles:

**(a)** Recognition and enhancement of the value of the amateur service to the public as a voluntary noncommercial communication service, particularly with respect to providing emergency communications.

**(b)** Continuation and extension of the amateur's unique ability to enhance international goodwill.

**(c)** Encouragement and improvement of the amateur service through rules which provide for advancing skills in both the communication and technical phases of the art.

**(d)** Expansion of the existing reservoir within the amateur radio service of trained operators, technicians, and electronics experts.

**(e)** Continuation and extension of the amateur's unique ability to advance the radio art.

---

### The Five Purposes Summarized

The five purposes of §97.1 establish amateur radio as:
1. An emergency communications resource
2. A tool for international goodwill
3. A pathway for technical skill development
4. A reservoir of trained communications personnel
5. A vehicle for advancing radio technology

These purposes are why amateur radio receives significant frequency allocations and why special emergency provisions (§97.401-407) exist. The emergency communication purpose is why amateur radio is valuable for disaster preparedness.

## Chapter 2: License Classes and Privileges

The FCC issues three classes of amateur radio operator licenses, each with different operating privileges. All classes authorize operation on VHF, UHF, and microwave bands. The classes differ primarily in HF (shortwave) privileges.

### 2.1 Technician Class

**Requirements:** Pass a 35-question multiple choice exam (Element 2).

**HF privileges (limited):**
- 80m band (3.525-3.600 MHz): CW only
- 40m band (7.025-7.125 MHz): CW only
- 15m band (21.025-21.200 MHz): CW only
- 10m band (28.000-28.500 MHz): CW, phone, and data
- 10m band (28.300-28.500 MHz): Phone allowed

**VHF/UHF/Microwave privileges (full):**
- All amateur frequencies at 50 MHz and above
- 6m (50-54 MHz), 2m (144-148 MHz), 1.25m (222-225 MHz), 70cm (420-450 MHz), 33cm (902-928 MHz), 23cm (1240-1300 MHz), and all microwave bands

**Practical note for emergency preparedness:** Technician class is sufficient for local and regional emergency communications on 2m and 70cm (the bands used by most emergency nets, repeaters, and ARES/RACES operations). Many emergency preparedness-focused hams remain Technician for years.

---

### 2.2 General Class

**Requirements:** Pass Element 2 (Technician) and Element 3 (35 questions).

**Additional HF privileges beyond Technician:**
- 80m (3.800-4.000 MHz): Phone and CW
- 40m (7.175-7.300 MHz): Phone and CW
- 30m (10.100-10.150 MHz): CW and data
- 20m (14.025-14.150, 14.225-14.350 MHz): Phone, CW, and data
- 17m (18.068-18.168 MHz): Phone, CW, and data
- 15m (21.025-21.200, 21.275-21.450 MHz): Phone, CW, and data
- 12m (24.890-24.990 MHz): Phone, CW, and data
- 10m (full phone and data)

**Why General matters for emergency comms:** HF bands (especially 40m and 20m) provide hundreds to thousands of miles of communication range without repeater infrastructure. In a widespread disaster that disables local repeaters, HF is often the only way to communicate regionally or nationally. For emergency preparedness, upgrading to General is highly recommended.

---

### 2.3 Amateur Extra Class

**Requirements:** Pass Elements 2, 3, and 4 (50 questions for Element 4).

**Privileges:** All amateur radio privileges on all amateur frequencies.

Additional HF sub-band access not available to General:
- CW-only portions of 80m, 40m, 20m, 15m bands
- Some data/RTTY sub-bands
- Certain limited exclusive-to-Extra segments of each major HF band

---

### 2.4 License Term and Renewal

**License term:** 10 years from date of grant.

**Renewal:** File online through the FCC Universal Licensing System (ULS) at wireless.fcc.gov/uls. Renewal is free. The exam is NOT required for renewal.

**Grace period:** The FCC allows a 2-year grace period after expiration during which the license can be renewed without re-examination. Operating during the grace period (after expiration but before renewal) is not permitted.

**Examination:** Exams are administered by Volunteer Examiner Coordinators (VECs). The ARRL VEC and other VECs coordinate exam sessions across the country. Exam fee is set by the VEC; typically $10-15. The FCC no longer charges a license application fee for Technician exams (a $35 FCC administrative fee applies to General/Extra upgrades as of 2022 — verify current fee schedule at fcc.gov).

**License lookup:** Any FCC license can be looked up at wireless.fcc.gov/uls using call sign or name.

## Chapter 3: Call Sign Structure

FCC-issued amateur call signs follow a structured format defined by district, sequential letter group, and class.

### 3.1 Call Sign Format

US amateur call signs follow the format: [prefix][number][suffix]

- **Prefix:** 1-2 letters identifying the US (K, W, N, or AA-AL series)
- **Number:** 0-9 indicating the approximate geographic district
- **Suffix:** 1-3 letters distinguishing the individual station

**Call sign groups by license class availability:**

| Group | Format | Available to |
|-------|--------|-------------|
| Group A | 1x2 (K1AB) | Extra only |
| Group B | 2x2 (KA1AB) | Advanced (grandfathered), Extra |
| Group C | 1x3 (K1ABC) | Tech/General/Extra |
| Group D | 2x3 (KA1ABC) | Tech/General/Extra |

Shorter call signs (Group A: 1x2) are generally more desirable for morse code operation (fewer characters to send). Extra class licensees can apply for Group A and B call signs through the Vanity Call Sign program.

## Chapter 4: Frequency Allocations

### 4.1 Key Bands for Emergency Preparedness

**2 meters (144-148 MHz) — Primary local emergency band**
The most widely used amateur band for local and regional communication. Requires only a Technician license. Most communities have multiple repeaters on 2m.
- National Simplex Calling Frequency: 146.520 MHz (FM voice) — the standard "call channel"; monitor this when traveling
- Standard repeater frequency pairs: offset +600 kHz (e.g., input 146.025, output 146.625)
- Emergency/disaster: Many ARES and RACES groups coordinate on 2m simplex and/or local repeaters

**70 centimeters (420-450 MHz) — Secondary local emergency band**
Similar to 2m but shorter range per watt. Excellent in urban environments. Supports digital modes well.
- National Simplex Calling Frequency: 446.000 MHz (FM voice)
- Standard repeater offset: +5 MHz

**40 meters (7.000-7.300 MHz) — Regional emergency HF band (General required)**
Daytime: 200-500 mile range (skywave). Nighttime: 1000+ miles. Best all-purpose regional HF band.
- ARRL/ARES recommendation: 7.200-7.300 MHz for phone
- 40m is affected by interference from international shortwave broadcasters at night
- Best range at dawn and dusk

**20 meters (14.000-14.350 MHz) — Long-distance HF band (General required)**
Daytime: national to international range. Best band for reliable long-distance contact during daylight.
- ARRL emergency phone: 14.300 MHz (MARAC/Maritime Mobile Net monitoring)
- 20m is less useful for regional comms but excellent for connecting to national emergency coordination

**80 meters (3.500-4.000 MHz) — Regional and local HF (General for phone)**
Best for medium-range (50-500 miles) contact in the evening and night. Heavily used for local nets.
- ARES traffic nets often operate on 80m phone (3.9-4.0 MHz region)
- More atmospheric noise than 40m/20m but excellent coverage within a region at night

### 4.2 Band Plan Summary

| Band | Frequency Range | Range (typical) | License (phone) | Key use |
|------|----------------|-----------------|-----------------|---------|
| 10m | 28.0-29.7 MHz | 1-1000s miles (cycle-dependent) | Technician | DX in solar max |
| 15m | 21.0-21.45 MHz | 500-5000 miles | General | DX daytime |
| 20m | 14.0-14.35 MHz | 500-10,000 miles | General | National/international DX |
| 40m | 7.0-7.3 MHz | 50-2000 miles | General | Regional emergency |
| 80m | 3.5-4.0 MHz | 50-500 miles night | General | Regional evening nets |
| 2m | 144-148 MHz | 10-100+ miles (repeater) | Technician | Local emergency |
| 70cm | 420-450 MHz | 5-50 miles (repeater) | Technician | Local emergency/digital |

## Chapter 5: Operating Rules

### §97.119 — Station Identification

One of the most consistently tested and enforced rules in Part 97.

**Required:** Transmit your call sign at the beginning and end of each communication, and at least every 10 minutes during a communication.

**Modalities:**
- Phone (voice): Announce call sign in plain language.
- Morse code (CW): Send call sign in international Morse.
- Digital modes: Identify in a mode receivable by the general public (usually also send CW or include spoken ID when beginning a digital session).

**Language:** Identification must be in English (for US stations). International phonetic alphabet is acceptable.

**Third-party communications:** If you allow a non-licensed person to speak through your station, you must be present at the controls and identify before and after their transmission. (§97.115)

---

### §97.113 — Prohibited Transmissions

**You may NOT transmit:**
- Music (except incidentally as part of a demonstration)
- Messages in codes or ciphers intended to obscure meaning (encryption is prohibited for most communications; digital modes are allowed if the specification is publicly available)
- Broadcasting to the general public (amateur radio is two-way communication, not broadcasting)
- Communications for hire or business purposes
- Obscene or indecent language
- False or deceptive signals or communications

---

### §97.101 — General Standards

**Frequency selection:** Use the minimum power necessary to accomplish the desired communication. Higher power is not inherently better — it causes more interference to others. Use the minimum effective power.

**Courtesy:** Amateur radio depends on shared frequency use. Proper etiquette:
- Listen before transmitting. Ensure the frequency is not already in use.
- If on a frequency, announce "Is this frequency in use?" before beginning a long communication.
- Yield to emergency traffic.

---

## Chapter 6: Emergency Communications

### §97.401 — Operation of a Station in Distress

**Any station may use any means of radio communication at its disposal to assist another station in distress, or to provide communications in a genuine emergency, regardless of frequency, mode, or power limitations.**

This is the critical emergency provision of Part 97. In a genuine emergency, the normal restrictions on frequencies, power levels, and even license class privileges do not apply. A Technician-class operator with a 2m radio may operate on HF frequencies if that is what is needed to summon help.

---

### §97.403 — Safety of Life and Protection of Property

**When immediate action is required to assist in saving life or property, an amateur station may communicate with any station, regardless of service (i.e., not just with other amateur stations), on any frequency, using any mode.**

This provision explicitly permits amateur operators to contact Coast Guard on maritime frequencies, emergency services on public safety frequencies, or any other service when life is at risk. The normal restriction to "amateur-only" frequencies is suspended in a genuine emergency.

---

### §97.405 — Station in Distress

**When a station is in distress:**

A station in distress may:
- Transmit on any frequency available
- Interrupt any ongoing communication
- Use any available mode and power

The distress signal for voice communications is "MAYDAY MAYDAY MAYDAY" (from French *m'aider* — help me). Repeat three times, followed by identification, position, and nature of distress.

For CW, the distress signal is SOS (···---···) sent continuously.

---

### §97.407 — Radio Amateur Civil Emergency Service (RACES)

RACES is a radio communications system established to provide amateur radio communication during emergencies for civil defense purposes. RACES stations operate under the auspices of state and local emergency management agencies and FEMA.

**Key RACES provisions:**
- RACES operations are activated by the responsible civil defense organization.
- Only RACES-registered stations may participate in RACES operations during emergencies.
- A station may be registered in RACES and also serve in ARES (these are not mutually exclusive).
- During RACES activation, RACES stations may only communicate with other RACES-registered stations and designated government stations.

**How to join RACES:** Contact your county or municipal emergency management agency. Registration is straightforward and usually requires proof of FCC license.

---

### Amateur Radio Emergency Service (ARES)

ARES (Amateur Radio Emergency Service) is a voluntary organization administered by the American Radio Relay League (ARRL). Unlike RACES (which is federally defined in Part 97), ARES is an ARRL-organized volunteer program that provides emergency communication support to agencies such as:
- FEMA, state, and local emergency management
- National Weather Service (SKYWARN spotters)
- American Red Cross (note: the Red Cross uses ARES volunteers but ARES itself is ARRL-organized)
- CERT and other disaster response agencies
- Hospitals and medical facilities

**How to join ARES:** Contact your local ARRL Section Emergency Coordinator (SEC). Find your section at arrl.org/ares. Membership requires a valid FCC amateur license.

**ARES vs. RACES:**
- ARES is voluntary and ARRL-organized; operates routinely (not only during declared emergencies)
- RACES is federally defined in Part 97; activates specifically for civil defense during declared emergencies
- Most active emergency communications volunteers are registered in both

---

### Emergency Nets

**ARRL National Traffic System (NTS):**
A nationwide network of amateur radio stations that relay message traffic from origin to destination across the country. The NTS operates on scheduled frequencies and times. Traffic is passed from local to section to regional to national level. Messages are formatted as "radiograms."

NTS net frequencies (sample, varies by region):
- 3.910 MHz (80m LSB) — early morning
- 7.290 MHz (40m LSB) — daytime
- 14.275 MHz (20m USB) — daytime
- Local/section nets vary by state; check arrl.org/nts for current schedule

**SKYWARN:**
SKYWARN is a cooperative program between amateur radio operators and the National Weather Service (NWS). SKYWARN spotters receive training to identify and report severe weather (tornadoes, hail, high winds, flash flooding) to the local NWS office.

Activation: Local NWS offices activate SKYWARN nets when severe weather is expected. Spotters deploy to safe locations and report observed conditions by radio.

To become a spotter: Attend a free NWS SKYWARN training session (offered regionally; check weather.gov/skywarn). Amateur radio is strongly encouraged but not required.

**SATERN (Salvation Army Team Emergency Radio Network):**
Amateur radio network that supports Salvation Army emergency operations. Provides communication for disaster relief activities.

## Chapter 8: Q Codes

Q codes are three-letter abbreviations developed for Morse code (CW) operation that allow rapid communication of common questions and answers. They are still widely used in voice operation as shorthand.

| Q Code | As a question | As an answer/statement |
|--------|--------------|----------------------|
| QRM | Is there interference? | There is interference |
| QRN | Are you troubled by static? | I am troubled by static |
| QRO | Should I increase power? | Increase your power |
| QRP | Should I decrease power? | Decrease your power |
| QRQ | Should I send faster? | Send faster |
| QRS | Should I send more slowly? | Send more slowly |
| QRT | Should I stop transmitting? | Stop transmitting |
| QRU | Do you have any messages for me? | I have no messages |
| QRV | Are you ready? | I am ready |
| QRX | When will you call again? | I will call you again at ___ |
| QRZ | Who is calling me? | You are being called by ___ |
| QSB | Is my signal fading? | Your signal is fading |
| QSL | Do you acknowledge? | I acknowledge (or: QSL card = confirmation card) |
| QSO | Can you communicate with ___? | I can communicate directly with ___ |
| QSY | Should I change frequency? | Change frequency to ___ |
| QTH | What is your location? | My location is ___ |
| QTR | What is the time? | The time is ___ |

**Usage example:** "QTH is Boston, Massachusetts" means "My location is Boston, Massachusetts." "QSY to 146.52" means "Change frequency to 146.52 MHz."

**QSL card:** A paper or electronic confirmation card that hams exchange to confirm two-way contact. Sent after a radio contact. Collected as a hobby, required for many operating awards.

## Chapter 9: Basic Antenna Theory

### 9.1 The Dipole Antenna

The dipole is the most fundamental antenna in amateur radio. Understanding it provides the foundation for all other antenna concepts.

**Structure:** A dipole consists of two equal-length conductors (wire, tubing) fed in the center, each extending in opposite directions. Total length = one half-wavelength at the operating frequency.

**Half-wavelength formula:**
```
Length (feet) = 468 / frequency (MHz)
Length (meters) = 143 / frequency (MHz)
```

Examples:
- 2m (146 MHz): 468/146 = 3.2 feet total (1.6 feet per side)
- 40m (7.200 MHz): 468/7.2 = 65 feet total (32.5 feet per side)
- 20m (14.200 MHz): 468/14.2 = 33 feet total (16.5 feet per side)

**Radiation pattern:** A horizontal dipole has maximum radiation broadside (perpendicular to the wire) and minimum radiation off the ends. Think of a donut shape — most signal goes out the sides, least off the ends.

**Feed impedance:** At resonance, a dipole has approximately 73 ohms impedance at the feed point. This is close to 50-ohm coaxial cable (the most common feed line), so direct connection works for many practical purposes.

**Practical installation:**
- Mount as high as possible — height above ground significantly affects performance
- Keep the ends away from metal objects and structures
- Use a balun (balanced-to-unbalanced transformer) at the feed point to reduce common-mode current on the coax shield
- Inverted-V configuration (center high, ends lower) requires only one high support and is nearly as effective as a horizontal dipole

---

### 9.2 The Quarter-Wave Vertical Antenna

A vertical antenna is fed at the base and requires a ground plane (radials or actual earth ground) for the "missing" half of the dipole.

**Length formula:**
```
Length (feet) = 234 / frequency (MHz)
```

Example: 2m vertical = 234/146 = 1.6 feet (about 19 inches)

**Radiation pattern:** A vertical over a good ground plane radiates omnidirectionally in the horizontal plane — equal signal in all directions. This makes it ideal for local communication (you don't know in which direction to aim).

**Ground planes:** A quarter-wave vertical requires a return path for RF current. This is provided by:
- Metal vehicle roof or body (mobile operation)
- Radials: wires or tubing extending horizontally from the base (minimum 3-4 radials at 90-degree spacing)
- Earth ground (effective but requires the antenna to be at ground level)

**The rubber duck antenna** on a handheld transceiver is a shortened, loaded vertical. Its performance is significantly worse than a proper quarter-wave vertical but acceptable for local communication.

---

### 9.3 Antenna Gain

Gain is a measure of how an antenna concentrates radiation in a particular direction compared to a reference antenna. Gain is not free power — it is focus.

**dBi:** Gain measured relative to an isotropic radiator (a theoretical antenna that radiates equally in all directions).

**dBd:** Gain measured relative to a dipole. 0 dBd = 2.15 dBi (a dipole has 2.15 dBi gain by concentrating energy in a plane rather than a sphere).

**Practical examples:**
- Isotropic antenna: 0 dBi (theoretical reference)
- Dipole: 2.15 dBi (0 dBd)
- Quarter-wave vertical over perfect ground: approximately 5 dBi
- Yagi (3-element): approximately 8 dBi
- 10-element Yagi: approximately 12-15 dBi

**Understanding gain:** A 3 dB gain increase doubles the effective radiated power in the favored direction. A 10 dB Yagi is equivalent to 10x the transmitter power in the direction the antenna points, at the cost of reduced radiation in all other directions.

**For emergency use:** An omnidirectional antenna (vertical) is usually preferred when you do not know the direction of the receiving station. A directional antenna (Yagi, dish) is used for long-distance point-to-point links or satellite communication.

---

### 9.4 SWR — Standing Wave Ratio

SWR (Standing Wave Ratio) measures how well the transmitter, feed line, and antenna are matched in impedance.

- **SWR 1:1** — Perfect match. All power from the transmitter is transferred to the antenna.
- **SWR 1.5:1** — Excellent. Less than 4% power loss. Indistinguishable from perfect in practice.
- **SWR 2:1** — Acceptable. About 11% power loss. Most modern transceivers handle this without issue.
- **SWR 3:1** — Elevated. About 25% power loss. Many radios will reduce power automatically at this level.
- **SWR > 5:1** — High. Significant power loss and potential stress on the transmitter output stage.

High SWR does not necessarily mean the antenna is bad — it means the impedance is mismatched. An antenna tuner (matching network) can correct SWR at the cost of some additional loss. The goal is always to design the antenna for resonance at the operating frequency, which naturally produces low SWR.

## Appendix B: RST Signal Report System

The RST (Readability, Signal Strength, Tone) system is used to report signal quality.

**R — Readability (1-5):**
1. Unreadable
2. Barely readable, occasional words
3. Readable with difficulty
4. Readable with no difficulty
5. Perfectly readable

**S — Signal Strength (1-9):**
1. Faint, barely perceptible
2. Very weak
3. Weak
4. Fair
5. Fairly good
6. Good
7. Moderately strong
8. Strong
9. Extremely strong

**T — Tone (1-9, for CW only):**
(Not used for voice; voice reports omit the T: "59" not "599")
1. Extremely rough
5. Musically modulated note, slight trace of ripple
9. Perfect tone, no trace of ripple or modulation

**Common reports:**
- "59" — Full readability, strong signal (voice)
- "599" — Perfect readability, strong signal, pure tone (CW)
- "33" — Weak, difficult to copy (voice); reported when conditions are poor
